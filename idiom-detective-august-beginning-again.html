Idiom Detective: The Case of Beginning Again

Five languages, five ways of saying “back to the start” — and the one detail each of them hides.

J Written by JY DM
📅 2026-08-01 ⏱️ 3 min read B1–C2

The case: every language has a phrase for starting over, and none of them mean the same thing.

French: reprendre à zéro — to take it up again from zero. The verb is reprendre, to take back, which assumes you once held it. Italian: ricominciare da capo — to begin again from the head, from the top of the page. Russian: начать с чистого листа — to begin from a clean sheet, the sheet you tore out. Greek: ξαναρχίζω από την αρχή — I begin again from the beginning, with the repetition built into the verb. English: back to square one, which is a board game, not a page.

Read them together and the languages disagree about what a beginning is: a page, a game, a possession you lost.

A detective's rule: do not trust the translation, trust the picture. Ask what the phrase would look like if you drew it. Clean sheet gives you paper. Square one gives you a board. Da capo gives you a page you have already turned. The picture tells you what the language believes about time — whether a beginning is a return, a reset, or a small humiliation — and that belief will show up in how people use the phrase about themselves.

Case notes

  • B1–B2: Which of the five images matches how you actually start again?
  • B1–B2: Translate “back to square one” into your language. What image did you use, and what does it assume?
  • C1–C2: The French phrase assumes you once held the thing. Does that make returning easier or harder than starting from nothing?
  • B1–B2: Draw one of the five phrases. Show it to your partner without saying which language it comes from.
