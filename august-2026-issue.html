— JY DM, Founder & Editor-in-Chief

A school is not a brick building with fluorescent lights, squeaking chalk, and the dread of being called to the blackboard unprepared. A school is any room—real or digital—where two curious humans exchange ideas without fear of humiliation. In this inaugural August issue, Get Ready for School, we strip away institutional intimidation and return to the biological core of communication: wonder, play, human warmth, and speaking from lesson one.

L'Édito: Editorial Dispatch — Foundations & Beginnings

Every August carries a quiet resonance: sharpened pencils, blank notebook grids, and the crisp smell of September around the corner. For adult learners, that feeling often arrives tethered to a knot in the stomach—the ghost of childhood tests and red ink. At COSY, we believe that beginnerhood is not a deficit; it is the most creative, plastic state your brain will ever inhabit.

In Conversation: Thematic Founder Interview with James (JY DM) — Part I: The Fear of Lesson One

COSY Gazette: James, why do so many adults who run companies, manage surgical teams, or raise children suddenly panic when asked to say 'Je m'appelle...' in an introductory language lesson?

JY DM: Because adults have spent decades curating a competent social identity. When you step into a new language at A0, that identity is instantly stripped away. You have the intellect of a forty-year-old and the vocabulary of an eighteen-month-old. Traditional schooling taught us that mistakes are moral failures penalized with bad grades. Our first pedagogical job at COSY is unlearning that trauma. Speaking first isn't a party trick; it is proof of life.

I Couldn't Help But Wonder: Why Do Adults Freeze at the Classroom Door?

Standing before a new teacher or a blank journal page, our heartbeat accelerates. Is it the grammar tables, or is it the fear of being seen as foolish? When children learn to ride a bicycle, scraping their knees is celebrated as evidence of trying. When an adult mispronounces a subjunctive vowel, they apologize as if they have committed a social transgression. Why do we grant ourselves so little permission to tumble?

DEPARTMENT II: FASHION & TRENDS

Debatable & Relatable: Typing vs. Handwriting in the Modern Classroom

As tablets and AI note-takers flood university lecture halls and language schools, the tactile intimacy of pen and paper faces an existential trial. We invited our international editorial team to outline the debate:

  • PRO Handwriting (The Cognitive Engram): 1. Functional MRI studies demonstrate that forming cursive letterforms activates sensorimotor neural circuits linked to deep lexical retrieval. 2. Notebooks have no push notifications, Slack banners, or battery alerts. 3. Tangible paper creates spatial memory—you remember a vocabulary word because it lived on the bottom-right corner of a yellow ruled sheet.
  • PRO Typing (The Modern Communicative Velocity): 1. Typing enables immediate synchronicity across global remote learning communities and instant cloud glossaries. 2. Accessibility tools allow dyslexic and visually-impaired students equalized participation. 3. Real-world corporate and academic work occurs on keyboards; learning to type fast in Cyrillic or Greek script is an indispensable modern survival skill.

Primary Research Field: Educational Neurobiology & Cognitive Ergonomics.
💡 Research Spark: Do tactile resistance differences between fountain pen nibs and glass touchscreens measurably alter long-term verb retention in elderly bilinguals?

Words Desk: The 3 Methodologies Across 14 Languages

How do you build a lexicon that stays with you forever? We apply the three foundational COSY methodologies: Pillar 1: The Root-Family Expansion (identifying shared Indo-European and Turkic cognate stems); Pillar 2: The Collocation Spine (never learning an isolated noun without its verb partner); and Pillar 3: The Communicative Anchor (immediately deploying the word within an autobiographical sentence within sixty seconds). Available across all 14 official COSY editions: EN, FR, IT, RU, EL, TT, BA, BR, PT, ES, DE, HY, KA, and CV.

Keeping Up with Science: Multilingual People Have Younger Brains

Recent longitudinal neuroimaging studies from Edinburgh and Geneva show that active daily code-switching delays cognitive symptom onset of neurodegenerative conditions by four to five years. The constant executive control required to suppress language A while speaking language B acts like high-intensity interval training for the prefrontal cortex.

Primary Research Field: Gerontolinguistics & Neuroplasticity.
💡 Research Spark: Can introductory conversational Greek or Russian acquired after age 65 reverse age-related white matter microstructural decay?

DEPARTMENT III: STORIES & VOICES

In Conversation: Thematic Founder Interview with James (JY DM) — Part II: Reclaiming Childhood Curiosity

COSY Gazette: In this issue, you wrote extensively about early beginnings. What did your own earliest school desk look like, and how did it shape the COSY vision?

JY DM: I remember wooden desks scratched with compass points and heavy grammar compendiums where we were asked to memorize conjugations before we had ever heard the music of the language spoken by a living person. Years later, sitting in an open-air café in Athens listening to elders debate politics over Greek coffee, I understood that language is theater, intimacy, and presence. That is why COSYmagazine treats language learning not as homework, but as an aesthetic magazine experience.

Mind Matters: Imposter Syndrome of the First Day

Whether you are fourteen walking into high school French or sixty-two attending your first Russian group seminar, the self-critical voice is identical: Everyone else understands this faster than I do. Psychology calls this the illusion of asymmetric insight. You observe your own inner chaos while only witnessing the quiet, calm poker faces of your peers. The antidote is mutual vulnerability: the moment one student laughs at their own pronunciation slip, the entire room breathes.

Primary Research Field: Affective Social Psychology.
💡 Research Spark: How does teacher-initiated self-deprecation reduce cortisol levels in introductory adult language classrooms?

Inside, he slipped an Italian pocket dictionary with a note: Learn the words of strangers, Elena, so that you never meet an enemy in this world.' My Life With & Without: Fear of Evaluation

WITH Fear of Evaluation: You spend ten minutes rehearsing a three-word coffee order in Rome. Your palm sweats on the counter. When the barista replies with a rapid colloquial question, your brain shuts down, you nod blindly, and you leave with an espresso you didn't want.

WITHOUT Fear of Evaluation: You walk in, smile warmly, butcher the word for croissant, laugh with the barista, ask how they say it in local Roman dialect, and make a friend before 9:00 AM. Fluency is not error-free speech; fluency is uninhibited connection.

DEPARTMENT IV: LIVING & SALON

If You Were: A Kindergarten Teacher in Tokyo

Imagine stepping into a sunlit room in Meguro with fifteen four-year-olds who speak zero words of your mother tongue. You have no grammar worksheets and no shared meta-language. All you have are tone, rhythm, wide eyes, wooden blocks, and song. Within three weeks, the children understand your gestures and you understand their laughter. What would happen to our adult language classes if we discarded grammar meta-explanations and taught with that same uninhibited physical music?

Primary Research Field: Multimodal Communication & Embodied Cognition.
💡 Research Spark: Does incorporating physical tactile rhythm games in adult beginner grammar lessons enhance subjunctive acquisition rates?

Grammar Made Cosy: Comparative Aspect Across EN, FR, IT, RU, EL

Every language family treats 'beginnings' with a distinct psychological lens. In English, we say 'I began studying' (focusing on the dynamic initiation). In French, the passé composé 'j'ai commencé' seals the inception as a distinct event. In Italian, 'ho cominciato' carries the same warm narrative pulse. In Russian, the inchoative prefix за- transforms 'говорить' (to speak) into 'заговорить'—like a light switch flipping on inside the speaker's chest. In Greek, the aorist 'άρχισα' crystallizes the exact turning point. Grammars are not rulebooks; they are cultural philosophies of time.

3. Italian: Crostata di Marmellata (crumbly pasta frolla tart layered with tart apricot jam and lattice pastry).
