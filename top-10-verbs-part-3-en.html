Welcome to Part 3 of our core verb series for A0–A1 and A2 English learners! (Read Part 1 and Part 2 if you missed them). Here we cover 10 core decision-making, commerce, cognitive, social, and action verbs. All conjugation tables and verb forms are consultable on COSYtools.

Ten verbs are enough to shop, plan a meeting, share how you feel and send a message on your first day. They are also the verbs where learners most often mix up the structure: pay for, decide on, agree with, play the. The good news is that the mistakes follow patterns, and patterns can be learned.

Read this article out loud. For each verb, start with the table and say the forms without looking. Then read the three sentences in the 'Say aloud' section, hide them, and repeat them from memory, changing one word to talk about your own life. Finish with the mistake card: it shows the sentence many beginners say, and the one to say instead. Speak first, understand the rule second: it is faster and more natural.

“You do not learn a verb until you can say it with a person, a place and a preposition.”

The memory map: ten verbs, five functions

Instead of learning ten verbs one after another, sort them by function. When you want to say something, first ask: am I shopping, thinking, feeling, connecting, or communicating? The verb comes almost by itself.

  • Shopping & resources: choose / buy / pay, spend / waste. Money, time and choices.
  • Thinking & deciding: remember / forget, decide, agree / disagree. Memory, decisions and opinions.
  • Feeling & meeting: feel, meet. Your inner state and other people.
  • Text & messages: write / read / type, send / get. Words that travel.
  • Free time: play. Games, sport, music and roles.

The 10 Core Verbs in Depth (Part 3)

1. Choose / Buy / Pay

These three verbs follow one little story: you choose something, you buy it, and you pay for it. Choose is about a decision between options. Buy is about getting something in exchange for money. Pay is about handing over the money, and it behaves differently from the other two: you pay for a thing, but you pay a person or pay by card.

VerbPresentPastParticipleExample
choosechoose / chooseschosechosenShe chose the blue jacket.
buybuy / buysboughtboughtWe bought bread this morning.
paypay / payspaidpaidI have paid the bill.
-ing formchoosing, buying, payingAre you paying by card?
Structurepay for + thingpay + personpay by / in + methodPay in cash.
  • Choosing: 'choose a book', 'choose between two options', 'choose a colour'.
  • Buying: 'buy groceries', 'buy a ticket online', 'buy a present for a friend'.
  • Paying: 'pay by card', 'pay for dinner', 'pay the rent', and the fixed phrase 'pay attention'.

Say aloud

It is hard to choose between the red bag and the black bag.

Choose between + two things. The verb is irregular: choose, chose, chosen.

Context: You are shopping with a friend.

I bought a train ticket online, and I paid by card.

Buy and pay in one sentence: 'bought' is irregular, 'paid' is irregular too.

Context: You explain how you organised a trip.

Mini-dialogue: A: Are you ready to order? B: Yes, I'll choose the soup. A: Do you want to pay now or later? B: I'll pay now. Can I pay by card?

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

01

Pay for or pay to

✗ Not quite

'I paid the coffee.'

✓ Say this instead

'I paid for the coffee.'

Why this mistake happens. In many languages the money goes straight onto the object. In English the thing you buy needs 'for'.

The COSY rule. Pay for + thing. Pay + person (no 'for'): 'I paid the waiter'. Pay by + card or cash.

More examples
  • She paid for the tickets.
  • I paid the driver.
  • Can I pay by card?

❓ I will ___ for the pizza, you paid last time.

We 'pay for' a thing. 'Buy for the pizza' is not possible.

2. Remember / Forget

Memory verbs are short but tricky. Remember means that something is in your mind, or that you do not forget to do something. Forget means it left your mind. Both can be followed by a noun, by 'to + verb' or by '-ing', and the meaning changes: 'remember to lock the door' is a task for the future, 'remember locking the door' is a memory of the past.

VerbPresentPastParticipleExample
rememberremember / remembersrememberedrememberedI remember your face.
forgetforget / forgetsforgotforgottenHe forgot his umbrella.
Negativedon't remember / don't forgetdidn't rememberDon't forget your keys!
Structureremember + nounremember to + verbremember + -ingRemember to call me.
Opposite pairremember ↔ forgetremind (someone)Please remind me.
  • Names and facts: 'remember a name', 'remember a phone number', 'I can't remember the word'.
  • Tasks: 'remember to buy milk', 'don't forget to send the email'.
  • Objects: 'I forgot my password', 'she forgot her bag on the train'.

Say aloud

I remember your name, but I forgot your phone number.

Both verbs take a direct object, with no preposition.

Context: You meet a friend after a long time.

Remember to bring your passport tomorrow.

Remember to + verb = do not forget a future task.

Context: You give advice before a trip.

Mini-dialogue: A: Did you remember the tickets? B: Oh no, I forgot them at home! A: Don't worry. Do you remember the password for the app? B: Yes, I remember it. I can show the ticket on my phone.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

02

Forget or leave

✗ Not quite

'I forgot my phone at home.'

✓ Say this instead

'I left my phone at home.' (or 'I forgot my phone.')

Why this mistake happens. Many languages use one verb for 'forget' and 'leave behind'. English says 'forget' when you do not remember, and 'leave' when you name the place.

The COSY rule. Forget + thing (no place). Leave + thing + place: 'I left my keys on the table'.

More examples
  • I forgot my keys.
  • She left her bag in the taxi.
  • He forgot to lock the door.

❓ Please ___ to turn off the lights.

After 'please' we use the base form: 'remember to turn off the lights'.

3. Feel

Feel is one of the most useful verbs for talking about yourself. It describes how your body or mind is right now. After 'feel' we normally use an adjective, not an adverb: 'I feel happy', 'I feel tired', 'I feel cold'. It is a state, so we use the simple present for it: 'I feel better today'.

Pronounfeel (present)PastExample
I / you / we / theyfeelfeltI feel tired.
He / she / itfeelsfeltShe feels cold.
Past participlefeltI have felt better.
-ing formfeelingHow are you feeling?
QuestionHow do you feel?Do you feel OK?
  • Body: 'feel cold', 'feel hungry', 'feel sick', 'feel tired'.
  • Emotions: 'feel happy', 'feel nervous', 'feel proud', 'feel lonely'.
  • Wanting: 'feel like + noun / -ing': 'I feel like a coffee', 'I feel like going out'.

Say aloud

I feel tired today, but I feel much better after lunch.

Feel + adjective. The past form of feel is 'felt'.

Context: You answer a colleague who asks about your day.

Do you feel like going for a walk?

Feel like + -ing = would you like to?

Context: You invite a friend.

Mini-dialogue: A: How do you feel? B: I feel a bit sick. A: Do you feel hot or cold? B: I feel cold. A: Let's go home. You need to rest.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

03

Feel good or feel well

✗ Not quite

'I feel badly today.'

✓ Say this instead

'I feel bad today.'

Why this mistake happens. Learners add -ly to every adjective after a verb. But 'feel' links you to a state, so it takes an adjective, not an adverb.

The COSY rule. Feel + adjective: bad, good, tired, happy. 'Well' is also fine for health: 'I don't feel well'.

More examples
  • I feel great.
  • She feels nervous.
  • I don't feel well.

❓ How do you ___ today?

After 'do you', we use the base form: 'How do you feel?'

4. Meet

Meet is used when two people come together, by plan or by chance. It is also the verb of first introductions: 'Nice to meet you!'. It takes a direct object, so we say 'I meet my friend', not 'I meet with my friend' in simple situations (the 'with' version exists in American English for formal meetings).

Pronounmeet (present)PastExample
I / you / we / theymeetmetWe meet every Monday.
He / she / itmeetsmetShe meets her teacher at nine.
Past participlemetHave you met my brother?
-ing formmeetingI'm meeting Anna at five.
Nouna meetingI have a meeting at ten.
  • First time: 'Nice to meet you', 'Pleased to meet you', 'Meet my friend Sara'.
  • Arrangements: 'Let's meet at 5 PM', 'meet outside the cinema', 'meet up on Saturday'.
  • Chance: 'I met an old friend in the street', 'meet new people'.

Say aloud

Nice to meet you. I'm Tom, and this is my sister.

A polite fixed phrase for a first meeting.

Context: You are introduced at a party.

Let's meet at the station at half past six.

Let's meet + place + time.

Context: You make a plan with a friend.

Mini-dialogue: A: Hi, have we met before? B: I don't think so. I'm Maria. A: Nice to meet you, Maria. I'm Leo. Let's meet for coffee next week! B: Great idea.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

04

Meet or know

✗ Not quite

'I met her for five years.'

✓ Say this instead

'I have known her for five years.'

Why this mistake happens. In many languages the verb for 'meet' also works for 'know someone'. In English, 'meet' is a moment, not a long state.

The COSY rule. Meet = the first time or an event. Know = a long relationship: 'I have known her for years'.

More examples
  • I met her last year.
  • We know each other well.
  • I'll meet you at six.

❓ It was nice to ___ you.

At a first encounter we say 'nice to meet you'. 'Nice to know you' is not the usual phrase.

📌 First meetings in English

A handshake and a smile are the usual greeting at a first meeting, with 'Nice to meet you' as the answer from both people. Hugs and kisses on the cheek are less common between new acquaintances than in many countries. After the first meeting, 'Good to see you' is more natural than 'Nice to meet you'. Small talk about the weather, the journey or the weekend is a friendly way to start.

5. Decide

Decide is about reaching a decision: you think, then you choose. It can be followed by 'to + verb' ('I decide to stay'), by 'on' + a thing ('decide on a hotel') or used alone ('I can't decide'). Remember the important note: we do not 'decide a problem' in English. A problem is solved. A decision is made.

  • To do something: 'decide to stay', 'decide to learn English'.
  • To choose a thing: 'decide on a date', 'decide on a name'.
  • Common phrases: 'decide quickly', 'hard to decide', 'make up your mind', 'I haven't decided yet'.

Say aloud

I decided to learn English last year.

Decide + to + verb.

Context: You tell your story at a language school.

We need to decide on a restaurant for Friday.

Decide on = choose after thinking.

Context: You plan a group dinner.

Mini-dialogue: A: Have you decided? B: Not yet. I can't decide between the pizza and the pasta. A: Make up your mind! B: OK, I decide on the pasta.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

05

Decide a problem

✗ Not quite

'We must decide this problem.'

✓ Say this instead

'We must solve this problem.'

Why this mistake happens. The verb 'decide' in many languages (for example Russian 'решать') also means 'solve'. English splits the meanings.

The COSY rule. Solve a problem or a puzzle. Decide to do something. Make a decision.

More examples
  • She solved the puzzle.
  • He decided to call.
  • We made a good decision.

❓ It is hard to ___ between two jobs.

We decide between choices. We solve problems.

6. Write / Read / Type

Three verbs of text: write (create words by hand or on screen), read (take in the words) and type (write with a keyboard). Read is special in speaking: the base form is /riːd/, but the past and the participle are spelled the same and pronounced /rɛd/. This is a small trap, and one you can learn in one minute.

  • Reading: 'read a book', 'read aloud', 'read the instructions'.

Say aloud

I write an email to my teacher every Monday.

Write + object. Past form: wrote.

Context: You describe your weekly routine.

Please read the text aloud and type the answer.

Two imperatives in a classroom.

Context: Your teacher gives instructions.

Mini-dialogue: A: What are you doing? B: I'm writing a message. A: Can you read it to me? B: Sure. Wait, I need to type one more line.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

06

Read in the past

✗ Not quite

'Yesterday I readed a good book.'

✓ Say this instead

'Yesterday I read a good book.'

Why this mistake happens. Learners add -ed to every past verb. 'Read' is irregular: the spelling does not change, only the sound.

The COSY rule. Read (/riːd/) in the present, read (/rɛd/) in the past. Write: wrote, written.

More examples
  • I read it yesterday.
  • She wrote a poem.
  • I have written the report.

❓ Yesterday I ___ a letter to my aunt.

Simple past of write is 'wrote'. 'Written' needs 'have'.

7. Spend / Waste

Spend and waste both use up a resource: money, time or energy. The difference is judgement. Spend is neutral or positive ('I spend time with my family'). Waste is negative: you use too much, or you use it badly. The preposition is important: you spend money on something, and you spend time with a person or doing something.

VerbPresentPastParticipleExample
spendspend / spendsspentspentI spent an hour on it.

-ing formspending, wastingHe is spending a lot.
Structurespend money on + nounspend time + -ingI spend time reading.
Nouna waste of timeIt's a waste of money.

  • Time: 'spend time with family', 'spend a week in Italy', 'spend an hour studying'.
  • Waste: 'waste energy', 'waste water', 'waste food', 'it's a waste of time'.

Say aloud

I spend a lot of money on books and coffee.

Spend + money + on + thing.

Context: You talk about your habits.

We spent the weekend with my grandparents.

Spend + time + with + people.

Context: You tell a friend about your weekend.

Mini-dialogue: A: Why are you leaving the lights on? B: Oh, sorry! A: Don't waste energy. B: You're right. How much did you spend on the new lamp? A: Not much. I spent ten euros.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

07

Spend a holiday or pass

✗ Not quite

'I passed the weekend at home.'

✓ Say this instead

'I spent the weekend at home.'

Why this mistake happens. Many languages say 'pass the time'. In English, we spend time, and 'pass' is mostly for the exam or the salt.

The COSY rule. Spend time or money. Pass an exam or a person in the street. Take a trip.

More examples
  • I spent two hours reading.
  • She passed the exam.
  • He spent all his money.

❓ Please don't ___ water when you wash the dishes.

Waste = use too much. We 'waste water', not 'spend water'.

📌 Time is money

English has many sayings that compare time to money: 'spend time', 'save time', 'waste time', 'invest time'. This shows how English speakers often think about time, as something valuable you can run out of. A polite way to say you are short of time is 'I'm afraid I don't have much time', and an apology for taking someone's time is 'Sorry to waste your time'.

8. Play (all variations)

Play has several faces, and each one has its own little rule. With sports and games, there is no article: 'play football', 'play chess'. With musical instruments, we use 'the': 'play the guitar'. In theatre and film, we play a role or a part. And with children, play is simply fun: 'the kids play in the park'.

  • Music: 'play the guitar', 'play a song', 'play music'.
  • Roles and fixed phrases: 'play a key role', 'play a trick on someone', 'play it safe'.

Say aloud

I play football on Sundays and my brother plays the guitar.

Sport with no article; instrument with 'the'.

Context: You describe your family hobbies.

Can you play this song again? I love it.

Play also works for music from a device.

Context: You are with friends at home.

Mini-dialogue: A: Do you play any sports? B: I play basketball. And you? A: I play the violin, but I don't play sports. B: Let's play a game tonight!

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

08

Play the football

✗ Not quite

'I play the football.'

✓ Say this instead

'I play football.'

Why this mistake happens. Learners think of the article from their own language. In English, sports do not take 'the' after play.

The COSY rule. Play + sport (no article). Play the + instrument. Go + -ing for some activities: 'go swimming'.

More examples
  • She plays tennis.
  • He plays the drums.
  • We play cards.

❓ My sister ___ the piano very well.

We 'play' instruments. 'Plays the piano' with 'the'.

9. Agree / Disagree

Opinions need two verbs: agree and disagree. They usually take the preposition with for a person ('I agree with you') and on or about for a topic ('we agree on the plan'). Do not say 'I am agree', because 'agree' is already a verb in English. It is not an adjective.

Agree with + person. Disagree with + thing.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

09

I am agree

✗ Not quite

'I am agree with you.'

✓ Say this instead

'I agree with you.'

Why this mistake happens. In several languages the expression is 'to be in agreement' or 'to be agree', so learners copy the structure with 'be'.

The COSY rule. Agree is a normal verb: 'I agree', 'he agrees'. Never use 'am / is / are' before it.

More examples
  • I agree.
  • She doesn't agree.
  • They disagree with me.

❓ I don't ___ with you. I think it is a bad idea.

Negative present: 'I don't agree'. No 'am'.

📌 Disagreeing politely

In English, a direct 'No, you're wrong' can sound rude. Native speakers usually soften a disagreement: 'I see what you mean, but...', 'I'm not sure I agree', 'That's a good point, however...'. In a meeting, these phrases keep the conversation friendly. Using 'respectfully' or 'with respect' before 'disagree' shows politeness and is common in formal discussions.

10. Send / Get

Send goes away from you: you send a message, a parcel, a photo. Get comes to you: you get an email, a gift, a message. Get is one of the most frequent verbs in English and has many meanings: receive, become, arrive, buy, understand. At A0–A2 level, focus on three: receive, arrive and become.

10

Send or get a letter

✗ Not quite

'I sent a letter from my friend.'

✓ Say this instead

'I got a letter from my friend.'

Why this mistake happens. In many languages, one verb can mean both 'send' and 'receive' depending on context. In English, the direction is built into the verb.

The COSY rule. Send = away from you. Get / receive = towards you. Never use 'send' for something that arrives.

More examples
  • I sent a card to Anna.
  • She got a card from Tom.
  • He sent me a message.

❓ I didn't ___ your message. Can you send it again?

'Get' = receive. After 'didn't' use the base form: 'didn't get'.

📌 Texts, emails and etiquette

In English-speaking countries, it is common to reply to a message within a day, and to send a short 'thank you' after a gift or a favour. In work emails, a polite start ('Hi Anna,', 'Dear Mr Brown,') and an ending ('Best regards', 'Kind regards') are standard. Use 'Could you send me...?' instead of 'Send me...' to sound polite.

Grammar & Structure for A0–A1 / A2 Learners

• Simple Sentence Structures: Focus on Subject + Verb + Object. • Verb Prepositions: Note key prepositions like 'agree with', 'spend on', 'decide on'. • Conjugations: Check full present, past, and future tense tables on COSYtools.

Your 5-minute speaking routine

Five minutes a day, out loud, beat an hour of silent reading on Sunday. Here is a simple plan to repeat for one week: one minute per step, no more.

  1. Minute 1, warm-up: say the three forms of buy, pay, forget, feel, meet, write, spend and send without looking at the tables.
  2. Minute 2, three verbs, three sentences: choose three verbs and say one true sentence about your life for each: 'I spend...', 'I feel...', 'I agree with...'.
  3. Minute 3, mistake hunt: reread one 'Not quite' card and say the correct sentence three times, changing the subject each time.
  4. Minute 4, mini-dialogue: play both roles of a dialogue with 'Can I pay by card?', 'Do you remember...?', 'Did you get my message?', and record yourself on your phone.
  5. Minute 5, replay: listen to the recording and pick one sentence to keep for tomorrow. Write it on a note near your desk.

Your one-week action plan

Do not try to learn all ten verbs in one day. Give each day a small mission and keep it real: use your own life, your own money, your own friends.

  1. Day 1: order or buy something and say 'I choose...', 'I pay by card' aloud, even at home.
  2. Day 2: write three notes with 'remember to' and 'don't forget to' and stick them on your door.
  3. Day 3: tell someone how you feel, in three different words: 'I feel tired, nervous, happy.'
  4. Day 4: meet someone, in person or online, and say 'Nice to meet you' and one follow-up question.
  5. Day 5: read ten lines aloud, write five lines about them, and type one line on your phone.
  6. Day 6: check your week: what did you spend money and time on? Say it with 'spend... on'.
  7. Day 7: send a short message in English to a friend, and agree or disagree with their answer politely.
