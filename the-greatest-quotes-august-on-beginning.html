The Greatest Quotes: On Beginning

Four lines that survive being read aloud — and the one thing all of them leave out.

J Written by JY DM
📅 2026-08-01 ⏱️ 3 min read B1–C2

“The limits of my language mean the limits of my world.”

— Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921)

“One language sets you in a corridor for life. Two languages open every door along the way.”

— Frank Smith

“Learning another language is not only learning different words for the same things, but learning another way to think about things.”

— Flora Lewis

“If you talk to a man in a language he understands, that goes to his head. If you talk to him in his own language, that goes to his heart.”

— attributed to Nelson Mandela

Read together, these four lines agree on something: a language is not a subject, it is a territory. They disagree on almost everything else — Wittgenstein makes it a boundary, Smith a corridor, Lewis a second way of thinking, Mandela a door into someone else.

What all four leave out is the room. None of them mentions the ten minutes before a first lesson, the person deciding whether to speak, or the teacher who has to make that decision easier. This issue has been about that room: the founder’s, the student’s, the teacher’s, and yours.

The four lines also disagree about who is doing the work. Wittgenstein’s boundary is inherited; Smith’s corridor is chosen; Lewis’s second way of thinking is earned slowly; Mandela’s door is opened by someone else. Most learners need all four at different moments: the boundary to know what they are up against, the corridor to keep walking, the second way of thinking as the reward, and another person to open the door.

  • B1–B2: Which of the four is your reason for learning, this month? Say it in one sentence.
📌 This could be a good research question

Do learners who keep a written record of why they are learning — in their own words, revisited monthly — speak more often in class than learners who set only proficiency goals?
