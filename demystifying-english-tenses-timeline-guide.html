Before you start: a simpler way to see English tenses

Most learners meet tenses as one big table of 12 forms. The table is useful, but it hides a simple idea. A verb tells you three things at once: when something happens, which form the verb takes, and how the speaker sees the action. This guide adds one idea at a time, with a timeline for each form.

Choose your route

  • A2: Steps 1 to 3, then the quiz.
  • B1: Steps 1 to 5. Pay special attention to past simple versus present perfect.
  • B2: The whole guide, including the future and perfect progressive forms and the notes on speaking and writing.

The levels in the headings show where learners usually meet each form. They are a guide, not an exam rule.

Step 1 (A2): Time, tense and aspect

Time is reality: before now, now, or after now. Tense is the form of the verb. English verbs have a past form (walked) and a present form (walk), and we talk about the future with helpers such as will or going to. Aspect is how the speaker sees the action: simple (a fact or a whole event), progressive (in progress) or perfect (linked to another point in time).

The "12 tenses" in coursebooks are 3 times multiplied by 4 aspects: simple, progressive, perfect and perfect progressive. It is a convenient teaching label, not the only description of English. Some grammars, such as Huddleston and Pullum's, say English has two tenses and express the future in other ways. Both views help; use the one that makes your learning easier.

The 12 forms: 3 times and 4 aspects
AspectPastPresentFuture
SimpleI workedI workI will work
ProgressiveI was workingI am workingI will be working
PerfectI had workedI have workedI will have worked
Perfect progressiveI had been workingI have been workingI will have been working

Step 2 (A2): Time markers are hints

Words such as yesterday or tomorrow help you choose a form. Notice the difference between in 2010 (a finished time) and since 2010 (a time that continues until now).

Time markers and the forms they often go with
Time markerOften goes withExample
yesterday, two days ago, last week, in 2010Past simple (finished time)I met her in 2010.
since 2010, for three yearsPresent perfect (up to now)I have known her since 2010.
already, yet, just, ever, neverPresent perfectHave you ever been to Brazil?
right now, at the momentPresent progressiveI am studying at the moment.
tomorrow, next week, soonFuture formsWe will start next week.

Markers are hints, not rules. I'm leaving tomorrow uses a present form for a future plan, and I have lived here since 2020 uses a time marker with the present perfect.

Careful: verbs of state, such as know, like, want and understand, are rarely used in the progressive. Say I know the answer, not I am knowing the answer. In informal speech there are exceptions, so do not treat this as an absolute rule.

Step 5 (B1): The perfect forms

Perfect forms always link two points in time. The present perfect links the past to now. The past perfect links an earlier past event to a later past event. The future perfect looks back from a future deadline.

Present perfect: I have finished my assignment.

A past action with a link to now. Form: have / has + past participle

Past simple or present perfect?

A minimal pair: past simple and present perfect
SentenceWhat it tells you
I lived in London for two years.The period is finished. I no longer live there.
I have lived in London for two years.The period continues until now. I still live there.

Use the past simple when the time is finished (yesterday, in 2010, last year). Use the present perfect when the time is not mentioned or is still open (today, this week, since 2010, ever).

Tenses without time markers

Native speakers rarely add already, now or yesterday to every sentence. The form of the verb carries the meaning.

What the form tells the listener
Form and exampleWhat the listener understands
Present perfect: I've lost my library card.I have a problem now. I can't borrow books today.
Past simple: I lost my library card.A finished event. The card may be found or replaced already.
Past progressive: The snow was falling over the library.A background scene in a story.
Present progressive: I'm meeting Anna at six.A fixed arrangement for the future, without saying tomorrow.
Past perfect: The archivist had locked the doors.This happened first, before the main event in the story.

What do you really need in speech and in writing?

Everyday speech relies mostly on a small set of forms: present simple, past simple, present progressive, present perfect, and will or going to for the future. Very long forms, such as the future perfect progressive, are rare in conversation. This is a general pattern, not a figure from a specific study, so use it to set your priorities and not as a rule.

Quiz: choose the best form

❓ I ___ in Lyon for two years, and I still live here.

The situation continues until now, so use the present perfect: I have lived.

❓ She ___ me an hour ago.

An hour ago gives a finished time, so use the past simple.

❓ When I arrived, the film ___ already started.

The film started before I arrived, so use the past perfect: had started.

❓ I ___ here since nine o'clock.

Since nine o'clock shows duration up to now, so use the present perfect progressive.

❓ By 5 PM, I ___ the report.

By 5 PM is a future deadline, so use the future perfect: will have completed.

❓ I ___ the answer.

Know is a verb of state. It is normally used in the simple form.

❓ Which sentence means the speaker lives in London now?

The present perfect links the past to now. The past simple and past perfect are about finished periods.

Summary: a plan for learners

  1. Separate time, tense and aspect. This makes the 12 forms easier to understand.
  2. Use time markers as hints, not as rules.
  3. Start with the simple forms and the present perfect. Compare past simple with present perfect often.
  4. Add the progressive forms, then the perfect forms, and leave the long perfect progressive forms for last.
  5. Learn the forms in whole sentences and notice what each one tells the listener.

Further reading: Rodney Huddleston and Geoffrey Pullum, A Student's Introduction to English Grammar; Michael Swan, Practical English Usage.
