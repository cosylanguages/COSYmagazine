Page 2: BE — The little verb that does almost everything ✨

🗺️ Your learning route 🟢 A0–A1 · Start here: identity, age, feelings, descriptions, place, time. 🔵 A1–A2 · Build confidence: negatives, questions, possession, there is/are, everyday expressions. 🟣 A2+ · One step further: be as a helping verb. Keep this page as a reference—you do not need to memorise everything at once.

Meet be: am, is, are. It helps you say who someone is, how someone feels, where something is, what something is like, and what time or weather it is. English often needs be where another language may use a different structure. 💡 The trick is to learn the pattern with the meaning, not just the words.

01 · The three shapes of BE 🧩

SubjectFull formShort formExample
II amI’mI’m ready.
YouYou areYou’reYou’re early.
HeHe isHe’sHe’s my brother.
SheShe isShe’sShe’s at home.
ItIt isIt’sIt’s cold.
WeWe areWe’reWe’re busy.
TheyThey areThey’reThey’re students.

💡 Pattern to remember: the subject chooses the form: I am, he/she/it is, you/we/they are. Short forms are very common in conversation. Use full forms when you want emphasis or a more formal tone.

02 · Ten everyday jobs for BE 🌟

🟢 A · Identity and names — say who someone is or identify a thing. Pattern: subject + be + noun/name. I’m Maya. He’s my brother, Leo. This is my new book.

🟢 B · Jobs and roles — use a/an with a singular countable job. I’m an English teacher. She’s a doctor. He’s an IT specialist. ⚠️ Say a doctor / an actor, not she is doctor.

🟢 C · Age — English uses be, not have. I’m 20 (years old). My parents are 55. How old are you? 🚦 Not: I have 20 years. ✅ Say: I’m 20.

🟢 D · Nationality and origin — use a nationality adjective or be from + place. I’m French. He’s Spanish. We’re from Paris. 🔎 Nationality describes a person; from tells you their origin.

🟢 E · Descriptions and characteristics — be + adjective. My friend is tall and funny. The cake is delicious. My room is clean today. 💡 Adjectives do not take a plural -s: They are happy, not they are happies.

🟢 F · Feelings and physical states — say how someone feels or what state they are in. I’m happy to meet you. We’re hungry and thirsty. He’s tired today. 🗣️ Mini-dialogue: “Are you okay?” “Yes, I’m fine, thanks.”

🟢 G · Location and position — be + place/preposition. I’m at home. She’s in the kitchen. The keys are on the table. 📍 Useful place words: in the room · on the table · under the bed · next to the café · behind the house · in front of the school · between two shops · near the station · at work/school.

🟢 H · Time, weather and dates — English commonly uses it is for time and weather. It’s 11 o’clock. It’s cold and sunny. It’s Friday. Today is October 23. 💡 For dates, you can also say It’s October 23rd (especially in American English) or It’s the 23rd of October (common in British English).

🔵 I · Demonstratives and possession — this/that is for one thing; these/those are for more than one. This is my bag. That is her sister. These are our books. Those are my parents. ⚖️ Be vs have: This bag is mine identifies the owner; I have a car says what I own.

🔵 J · Material, measurements and numbers — be can describe what something is made of, its price, size or distance. The table is made of wood. The ticket is €10. The station is two kilometres away. My room is four metres wide. 🔎 In is made of, be is part of a passive structure—see “One step further” below.

03 · Make it negative 🚦

To make a negative, put not after am/is/are. You do not need do or does.

Full formShort formExample
am not’m notI’m not tired today.
is notisn’t / ’s notShe isn’t at school.
are notaren’t / ’re notThey aren’t hungry.

⚠️ Two common traps • Say I’m not or I am not—standard English does not use I amn’t. • ❌ She doesn’t be happy. ✅ She isn’t happy.

04 · Ask yes/no questions ❓

Move am/is/are before the subject: You are ready. → Are you ready?

StatementQuestionYes, …No, …
You are happy.Are you happy?Yes, I am.No, I’m not.
He is a doctor.Is he a doctor?Yes, he is.No, he isn’t.
She is here.Is she here?Yes, she is.No, she isn’t.
It is cold.Is it cold?Yes, it is.No, it isn’t.
They are ready.Are they ready?Yes, they are.No, they aren’t.

💡 Short-answer rule: in positive short answers, use the full form: ✅ Yes, she is. ❌ Yes, she’s. Negative short answers can use contractions: No, she isn’t.

05 · Ask for the detail: WH- questions 🔎

Question wordWhat it asksExample
Whatthing / informationWhat is your name?
Whoperson / identityWho is she?
WhereplaceWhere are your keys?
Whentime / dateWhen is your birthday?

✨ Two questions that look similar—but do different jobs: • What is your teacher like? → asks about personality or characteristics: She’s kind and funny. • How is your grandfather? → asks about health or well-being: He’s fine, thanks.

06 · Say that something exists: THERE IS / THERE ARE 🏡

Use there is for one thing and there are for more than one. It is a useful way to talk about what you can see or what is available.

MeaningPositiveNegativeQuestion
One thingThere is a chair.There isn’t a chair.Is there a chair?
More than oneThere are two chairs.There aren’t any chairs.Are there any chairs?

🗣️ In real life: Is there a bank near here? · There’s a café next to the station. · There are two free seats.

07 · Small words, big meaning: fixed expressions 💬

  • be right / be wrong — You’re right. I’m wrong.
  • be afraid of / be sure — She’s afraid of dogs. I’m sure it’s okay.
  • be interested in / be good at — He’s interested in music. She’s good at English.
  • be late / early / ready / busy / free — We’re late. Dinner is ready. I’m free tomorrow.

🔎 Notice the prepositions that belong with some expressions: interested in, good at, afraid of. Learn them as little word groups.

08 · One step further: BE can be a helping verb 🟣 A2+

So far, be has carried the main meaning: She is happy. Sometimes, be helps another part of the sentence instead. You can recognise these patterns now and learn them gradually.

PatternExampleWhat BE is doing
be + -ingShe is working.Helps form the present continuous: an action happening around now.
be + past participleThe table is made of wood.Helps form the passive: the sentence focuses on the thing or action, not the doer.

⚖️ Compare: She is happy = be describes her state. · She is working = is helps form the action verb working.

09 · Quick check 🎯

❓ Choose the correct sentence about age and a job.

Use be for age and put a/an before a singular countable job: She is 25. She is a doctor.

❓ Choose the correct positive short answer: “Is she at home?”

Positive short answers use the full form: Yes, she is.

❓ Complete the sentence: “___ there any cafés near here?”

Use are with the plural noun cafés: Are there any cafés near here?

❓ Which sentence describes an action happening around now?

In He is working, is + working forms the present continuous. In the other sentences, is describes a state or identity.

🌱 You do not have to master every pattern today. Start by noticing am / is / are, then practise one use at a time. The more examples you meet, the more natural BE will feel.

Page 3: Verb 2 — "To Have" (have / has)

The verb to have is one of the most essential verbs in English. At A0–A1 level, you use it to talk about possessions, family, physical characteristics, food and drink, daily activities, and health conditions.

There are two essential present simple forms to learn first: • have — I, you, we, they • has — he, she, it

1. Forms & Conjugation Table (Present Simple)

SubjectAffirmative (+)Negative (-)Question (?)
I / You / We / TheyI have a phone. / You have a nice house. / We have two children. / They have a new car.I don't have a car. / You don't have a pen. / We don't have time. / They don't have any pets.Do I have time? / Do you have a question? / Do we have class today? / Do they have tickets?
He / She / ItHe has a sister. / She has blue eyes. / It has four legs.He doesn't have a job. / She doesn't have a brother. / It doesn't have a name.Does he have a pet? / Does she have a car? / Does it have a camera?

⚠️ IMPORTANT CONJUGATION RULE: Use has only with he, she, and it in positive present simple sentences. • Correct: She has a cat. • Incorrect: She have a cat.

2. Main Usages & Sentence Frames

  • A. Possessions: I have a new laptop. / She has a red bicycle. / We have a big house in Paris. / They have a friendly dog.
  • B. Family & Relationships: I have two brothers. / She has a young daughter. / He has a big family. / We have many good friends.
  • C. Physical Characteristics: I have brown eyes. / She has long blonde hair. / He has a dark beard. / The cat has a long tail.
  • D. Things & Features: The apartment has three bedrooms. / The car has four doors. / My phone has a great camera. / The school has a large library.
  • E. Problems & Needs: I have a problem with my laptop. / She has a bad headache. / We have an urgent question. / He has a lot of work today.
  • F. Time & Availability: I have free time this afternoon. / We have an important meeting today. / She has an English lesson at 10 AM. / They have no money.
  • G. Food & Drink (Action Meaning): I have a delicious sandwich for lunch. / She has a cup of tea every morning. / We have breakfast at 8 o'clock. / They have dinner together at home. (Here have means eat or drink).

3. Negative Sentences (-) & The Base Verb Rule

To make negative sentences with have, use don't have or doesn't have.

SubjectNegative StructureExample Sentence
I / You / We / Theydon't haveI don't have any money. / We don't have class today.
He / She / Itdoesn't haveShe doesn't have a brother. / My phone doesn't have a good camera.

⚠️ VERY IMPORTANT RULE: After doesn't, always use the base form have, never has. • Correct: She doesn't have a car. • Incorrect: She doesn't has a car.

4. Yes/No Questions (?) & Short Answers

Question (?)Positive Short AnswerNegative Short Answer
Do you have a car?Yes, I do.No, I don't.
Does he have a brother?Yes, he does.No, he doesn't.
Does she have a job?Yes, she does.No, she doesn't.
Do they have pets?Yes, they do.No, they don't.

⚠️ QUESTION RULE: When you use does, the main verb returns to its basic infinitive form, have. • Correct: Does she have a cat? • Incorrect: Does she has a cat?

5. Wh- Questions with "Have"

  • What: What do you have in your bag? / What do you have for breakfast?
  • Who: Who do you have in your family?
  • How many: How many brothers do you have? / How many bedrooms does the house have?
  • How much: How much money do you have? / How much time do we have?
  • What colour: What colour eyes does she have?
  • What kind: What kind of phone does he have?

6. "Have got" vs "Do/Does Have"

Have got is common in informal English (especially British English) for possession, family, and physical features.

StructureDo/Does + Have SystemHave got System
Affirmative (+)I have a car. / She has a cat.I've got a car. / She's got a cat.
Negative (-)I don't have a dog. / She doesn't have a car.I haven't got a dog. / She hasn't got a car.
Question (?)Do you have a pet? / Does she have a car?Have you got a pet? / Has she got a car?
Short AnswerYes, I do. / No, she doesn't.Yes, I have. / No, she hasn't.

⚠️ NEVER MIX THE TWO SYSTEMS: • Correct: Does she have a car? OR Has she got a car? • Incorrect: Does she have got a car?

7. Everyday Expressions & Illnesses with "Have"

  • Daily Activities & Routines: have breakfast / lunch / dinner, have a coffee, have a shower / bath, have a break, have a rest, have a party, have a good time, have a look, have a chat, have a holiday.
  • Illnesses & Physical Conditions: have a headache, have a stomachache, have a cold, have a fever, have a toothache.
  • Countable vs Uncountable (some / any): • Affirmative (+): I have some apples. / I have some water. • Negative (-): I don't have any apples. / I don't have any money. • Question (?): Do you have any questions?

8. Common Mistakes & Side-by-Side Comparisons

Contrast these key structures with 'to be' and 'to have': • State / Feeling: I am hungry. (be + adjective) • Possession: I have a sandwich. (have + noun) • Activity: I have breakfast at 8 AM. (have + meal)

9. Check Your Understanding

❓ Choose the correct negative form: 'She ___ a brother.'

After 'doesn't', the main verb always returns to its base form 'have'. 'She doesn't have a brother' is correct.

❓ Which sentence correctly mixes 'have got' and 'do'?

Never mix 'do/does' with 'got'. Use 'Has she got a car?' or 'Does she have a car?'

Page 4: Verb 3 — "To Think"

To Think allows you to express your opinion, share your thoughts, and ask others for their view.

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Present Simple Forms: I/You/We/They think | He/She/It thinks.
  • Expressing Opinions (I think that...): I think it is a good idea. / I think learning English is fun. / She thinks this movie is great.
  • Short Responses: I think so. (I agree / I believe so) | I don't think so. (I disagree).
  • Asking Opinions: What do you think about this idea? / What do you think of this film?
  • Key Collocations: think twice before deciding (be careful), think about the problem (consider), think of a solution (have an opinion/idea).

Page 5: Verb 4 — "To Know"

The verb to know is essential for talking about facts, information, people, places, skills, and things we understand. In our logical sequence: first we think, and then we know!

The primary present simple forms are: • know — I, you, we, they • knows — he, she, it

1. Forms & Conjugation Table (Present Simple)

SubjectAffirmative (+)Negative (-)Question (?)
I / You / We / TheyI know the answer. / You know my brother. / We know this city well. / They know the truth.I don't know his name. / You don't know this place. / We don't know what to do. / They don't know anyone here.Do I know you? / Do you know the way? / Do we know the rules? / Do they know the price?
He / She / ItHe knows the teacher. / She knows the answer. / It knows the route.He doesn't know my address. / She doesn't know the truth. / It doesn't know any tricks.Does he know the time? / Does she know Peter? / Does it know where to go?

⚠️ IMPORTANT CONJUGATION RULE: Add -s to know only with he, she, and it in affirmative sentences. • Correct: She knows the answer. • Incorrect: She know the answer.

2. Facts, Information & Embedded Question Words

  • A. Facts & Direct Information (Subject + know/knows + noun): • I know the correct answer to question 5. • She knows his full name and phone number. • He knows the truth about what happened. • We know the safety rules at work.
  • B. Information with Question Words: • I know where he lives in London. • She knows what you want for your birthday. • We know when the English lesson starts. • They know why he is angry today.

3. Familiarity with People & Places

  • A. People: I know Anna from university. / She knows my older brother. / Do you know this man? / We don't know those people over there.
  • B. Places: I know this city very well. / He knows London like the back of his hand. / Do you know this Italian restaurant? / They don't know the area.
  • ⚠️ DISTINCTION: In English, knowing someone means you are familiar with them. • I know Peter. = I am familiar with him. • I meet Peter every day. = I encounter/see him every day. • I know Peter well. = I am very familiar with him.

4. Negative Sentences (-) & "I don't know"

Use don't know or doesn't know. After doesn't, always use the base verb know, never knows. • Correct: He doesn't know. • Incorrect: He doesn't knows.

The Essential Phrase — "I don't know": • "What's his name?" — "I don't know." • "Where is my phone?" — "I don't know." • Useful Variations: I really don't know. / I don't know yet. / I don't know why. / I don't know anything about it.

5. Yes/No & Wh- Questions (Embedded Word Order)

In embedded questions with Do you know...?, use statement word order (Subject + Verb). • Direct question: Where does he live? • Embedded question: Do you know where he lives? (Correct statement order: where he lives, NOT where does he live). • Do you know what time it is? (NOT what time is it). • Do you know who she is? (NOT who is she). • Do you know how old he is? / Do you know where the station is?

6. "Know that" & "Know how to" (Skills)

  • A. Know (that) + sentence: I know that you are tired. / She knows that he is French. / We know English is important. ('that' is optional in spoken English). • Negative contrast: I don't know that he is here. (lack of awareness) vs I don't know if he is here. (uncertainty).
  • B. Skills (know how to + base verb): • I know how to swim. / She knows how to cook Italian food. / He knows how to drive a car. / They don't know how to play chess.
  • C. Know vs Know about: • I know Maria. = I am acquainted with Maria personally. • I know about computers. = I have knowledge on the topic of computers.

7. Stative Verb Rule & Comparisons

⚠️ STATIVE VERB WARNING: Know describes a mental state, so it is not used in continuous tenses (-ing). • Correct: I know the answer. • Incorrect: I am knowing the answer. • To talk about gradual familiarity, say: I am getting to know him.

VerbMeaningExample Sentence
thinkHave an opinion / beliefI think he is right.
knowHave factual certainty or familiarityI know he is right.
understandGrasp the meaning of somethingI understand what he says.

8. Key Daily Expressions with "Know"

  • let someone know = inform me (Let me know when you arrive!)
  • get to know someone = gradually become familiar (I want to get to know you.)
  • as far as I know = to my knowledge (As far as I know, the café is open.)
  • know by heart = memorize completely (I know this poem by heart.)
  • know for sure = be 100% certain (I don't know for sure.)

9. Check Your Understanding

❓ Which sentence has correct word order after 'Do you know...?'

In embedded questions after 'Do you know...?', use normal statement word order: 'where the station is'.

❓ Choose the correct skill structure: 'She knows ___ driving a car.'

For skills, use 'know how to + base verb': 'She knows how to drive a car.'

Page 6: Verb 5 — "To Like" ❤️

After learning to be, to have, to think, and to know, let's master to like! This is a very useful verb for talking about things you enjoy, your hobbies, food, music, people, and preferences.

1. Preference Scale: Love, Like, Dislike & Hate

  • ❤️ Love (❤️❤️❤️❤️❤️): Enjoy very much ("I love chocolate!")
  • ❤️ Like (❤️❤️❤️): Enjoy ("I like chocolate.")
  • 👎 Dislike (👎): Not enjoy ("I dislike chocolate." — Note: Dislike sounds formal; in daily English, people usually say "I don't like chocolate.")
  • 😠 Hate (😠): Dislike very strongly ("I hate cold weather." — Note: Be careful using hate with people, as it sounds harsh).

2. Conjugation Table & The Third-Person Rule

To like is a regular verb. In the present simple, add -s for he, she, and it.

SubjectAffirmative (+)Negative (-)Question (?)
I / You / We / TheyI like music. / You like pizza. / We like traveling. / They like concerts.I don't like coffee. / You don't like tea. / We don't like waiting. / They don't like noise.Do I like this? / Do you like tea? / Do we like this movie? / Do they like cooking?
He / She / ItHe likes sports. / She likes reading. / It likes milk.He doesn't like cold weather. / She doesn't like cats. / It doesn't like water.Does he like football? / Does she like dancing? / Does it like warmth?

⚠️ IMPORTANT RULE: Add -s with he, she, and it in positive sentences. • Correct: She likes pizza. • Incorrect: She like pizza. In negatives and questions, after doesn't or does, the main verb returns to base like: • Correct: She doesn't like tea. / Does he like football? • Incorrect: She doesn't likes tea. / Does he likes football?

3. What Can Come After "Like"?

  • Pattern 1: Like + Noun: I like Italian food. / She likes dogs. / They like classical music.
  • Pattern 2: Like + Verb-ing: I like swimming in the morning. / He likes cooking pasta. / We like watching films. (Emphasizes active enjoyment)
  • Pattern 3: Like + to + Verb: I like to read before bed. / She likes to dance. / They like to travel in summer. (Emphasizes habit or preference)
  • ⚠️ DISTINCTION: • I like coffee. = I enjoy coffee generally. • I'd like a coffee. = I want a coffee now, politely. (Would like is covered on Page 7 with To Want!).

4. Related Verbs: Love, Dislike & Hate

The same grammar rules apply to love, dislike, and hate: • I love my family. / She loves dancing. • He dislikes waiting. (Or naturally: He doesn't like waiting.) • My cat hates water. / They hate getting up early.

5. Everyday Expressions & Stative Verb Rule

  • Intensity: I really like it! (A lot) | I quite like it. (To some degree) | I don't like it very much.
  • Polite Neutrality: I don't mind. (It is okay / no objection) | It's not my cup of tea. (Idiom: Not something I enjoy)
  • ⚠️ STATIVE VERB RULE: Like describes a state or preference, so use present simple in standard English (I like this song, NOT I'm liking this song).

6. Check Your Understanding

❓ Choose the correct sentence:

With 'she', use 'doesn't' followed by the base verb 'like' without an -s.

❓ What does 'I'd like a glass of water' mean?

'I'd like' means 'I would like', which expresses a polite desire right now, not a general hobby.

Page 7: Verb 6 — "To Want / Would Like"

To Want and Would Like express desires, intentions, and polite requests.

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Direct Desires (Want + Noun / Verb): I want a glass of cold water. / I want to learn English fluently. / Where do you want to go tonight?
  • Polite Standard (Would like + Noun / Verb): I would like to order lunch now, please. / She would like a fresh salad.
  • Negative Forms: I don't want to be late for class. / He doesn't want to go home yet.
  • Common Phrases: if you want to come, I want to know the truth, we would like to see the museum.

Page 8: Verb 7 — "To Go / Be Going To"

To Go expresses movement, travel, routines, and future intentions (be going to).

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Present Simple Forms: I/You/We/They go | He/She/It goes.
  • Movement & Prepositions: I go to work by train. / She goes to school every morning. / We go to the park on Sundays.
  • Exceptions without 'to': go home, go abroad on holiday, go downtown, go grocery shopping.
  • Future Intentions (be going to + verb): I am going to eat dinner with friends. / They are going to buy a new car next month.
  • Transport Phrases: go on foot (walk), go by bus, go by train, go by car.

Page 9: Verb 8 — "To Can" (Modal Verb)

Can is a modal verb expressing ability, permission, and polite requests.

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Modal Rule: Can never changes form (no '-s' for he/she/it). It is followed directly by a base verb without 'to'.
  • Ability: I can speak three languages. / She can swim very fast in the pool. / He can play the acoustic guitar.
  • Negative Ability (cannot / can't): I can't drive a manual car. / She can't come to the party tonight.
  • Requests & Permission: Can you help me with this bag? / Can I have a glass of water? / You can sit here near the window.
  • Useful Phrases: as fast as I can, I can do it, Can I ask a quick question?

Page 10: Verb 9 — "To Eat"

To Eat is used for food, daily meals, dining habits, and food preferences.

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Present Simple Forms: I/You/We/They eat | He/She/It eats.
  • Daily Food: I eat fresh fruit every morning for breakfast. / He eats lunch at 1 o'clock at work.
  • Questions & Desires: What do you want to eat for dinner? / Do you eat vegetarian food?
  • Phrasal Verbs & Collocations: eat out at an Italian restaurant, eat healthy food, let's find something to eat.

Page 11: Verb 10 — "To Drink"

To Drink covers beverages, hydration, offering drinks, and social café situations.

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Present Simple Forms: I/You/We/They drink | He/She/It drinks.
  • Daily Beverages: I drink two litres of water every day. / He drinks black coffee in the morning. / We drink hot tea in winter.
  • Offers & Polite Phrases: Would you like something to drink? / What would you like to drink with your meal?
  • Noun Usage: a hot drink on a cold day, a cold drink in summer, let's have a drink together.
