BE — The little verb that does almost everything ✨

🗺️ <strong>Your learning route</strong> &nbsp; <span aria-label="Level A0-A1 Beginner">🟢</span> <strong>A0–A1 · Start here:</strong> identity, age, feelings, descriptions, place, time. &nbsp; <span aria-label="Level A1-A2 Intermediate">🔵</span> <strong>A1–A2 · Build confidence:</strong> negatives, questions, possession, <em>there is/are</em>, everyday expressions. &nbsp; <span aria-label="Level A2+ Advanced">🟣</span> <strong>A2+ · One step further:</strong> <em>be</em> as a helping verb. Keep this page as a reference—you do not need to memorise everything at once.

Meet <strong>be</strong>: <em>am, is, are</em>. It helps you say who someone is, how someone feels, where something is, what something is like, and what time or weather it is. English often needs <strong>be</strong> where another language may use a different structure. 💡 The trick is to learn the pattern with the meaning, not just the words.

01 · The three shapes of BE 🧩

SubjectFull formShort formExample

💡 <strong>Pattern to remember:</strong> the subject chooses the form: <strong>I am</strong>, <strong>he/she/it is</strong>, <strong>you/we/they are</strong>. Short forms are very common in conversation. Use full forms when you want emphasis or a more formal tone.

02 · Ten everyday jobs for BE 🌟

🟢 A · Identity and names — say who someone is or identify a thing.
Pattern: subject + be + noun/name.
I’m Maya. He’s my brother, Leo. This is my new book.

🟢 B · Jobs and roles — use a/an with a singular countable job.
I’m an English teacher. She’s a doctor. He’s an IT specialist.
⚠️ Say a doctor / an actor, not she is doctor.

🟢 C · Age — English uses be, not have.
I’m 20 (years old). My parents are 55. How old are you?

INSTEAD OF ❌ Incorrect Form
TRY THIS ✅ Correct Form
I have 20 years.
I’m 20. (or I am 20 years old.)

🟢 D · Nationality and origin — use a nationality adjective or be from + place.
I’m French. He’s Spanish. We’re from Paris.
🔎 Nationality describes a person; from tells you their origin.

🟢 E · Descriptions and characteristics — be + adjective.
My friend is tall and funny. The cake is delicious. My room is clean today.
💡 Adjectives do not take a plural -s: They are happy, not they are happies.

🟢 F · Feelings and physical states — say how someone feels or what state they are in.
I’m happy to meet you. We’re hungry and thirsty. He’s tired today.
🗣️ Mini-dialogue: “Are you okay?” “Yes, I’m fine, thanks.”

🟢 G · Location and position — be + place/preposition.
I’m at home. She’s in the kitchen. The keys are on the table.
📍 Useful place words: in the room · on the table · under the bed · next to the café · behind the house · in front of the school · between two shops · near the station · at work/school.

🟢 H · Time, weather and dates — English commonly uses it is for time and weather.
It’s 11 o’clock. It’s cold and sunny. It’s Friday. Today is October 23.
💡 For dates, you can also say It’s October 23rd (especially in American English) or It’s the 23rd of October (common in British English).

🔵 I · Demonstratives and possession — this/that is for one thing; these/those are for more than one.
This is my bag. That is her sister. These are our books. Those are my parents.
⚖️ Be vs have: This bag is mine identifies the owner; I have a car says what I own.

🔵 J · Material, measurements and numbers — be can describe what something is made of, its price, size or distance.
The table is made of wood. The ticket is €10. The station is two kilometres away. My room is four metres wide.
🔎 In is made of, be is part of a passive structure—see “One step further” below.

03 · Make it negative 🚦

To make a negative, put <strong>not after am/is/are</strong>. You do not need <em>do</em> or <em>does</em>.

Negative conjugation forms table for verb BE
Full formShort formExample
am not’m notI’m not tired today.
is notisn’t / ’s notShe isn’t at school.
are notaren’t / ’re notThey aren’t hungry.
⚠️ Common Traps

• Say I’m not or I am not—standard English does not use I amn’t.

INSTEAD OF ❌ Incorrect Form
TRY THIS ✅ Correct Form
She doesn’t be happy.
She isn’t happy.

04 · Ask yes/no questions ❓

Move <strong>am/is/are</strong> before the subject: <strong>You are ready. → Are you ready?</strong>

Yes and No questions and short answers table for verb BE
StatementQuestionYes, …No, …
You are ready.Are you happy?Yes, I am.No, I’m not.
He is a doctor.Is he a doctor?Yes, he is.No, he isn’t.
She is here.Is she here?Yes, she is.No, she isn’t.
It is cold.Is it cold?Yes, it is.No, it isn’t.

💡 <strong>Short-answer rule:</strong> in positive short answers, use the full form: ✅ <mark class="example-chip">Yes, she is.</mark> &nbsp; ❌ <mark class="example-chip">Yes, she’s.</mark> Negative short answers can use contractions: <mark class="example-chip">No, she isn’t.</mark>

05 · Ask for the detail: WH- questions 🔎

✨ <strong>Two questions that look similar—but do different jobs:</strong> <br>• <strong>What is your teacher like?</strong> → asks about personality or characteristics: <mark class="example-chip">She’s kind and funny.</mark> <br>• <strong>How is your grandfather?</strong> → asks about health or well-being: <mark class="example-chip">He’s fine, thanks.</mark>

06 · Say that something exists: THERE IS / THERE ARE 🏡

Use <strong>there is</strong> for one thing and <strong>there are</strong> for more than one. It is a useful way to talk about what you can see or what is available.

There is and there are sentence patterns table
MeaningPositiveNegativeQuestion
One thingThere is a chair.There isn’t a chair.Is there a chair?
More than oneThere are two chairs.There aren’t any chairs.Are there any chairs?

🗣️ <strong>In real life:</strong> <mark class="example-chip">Is there a bank near here?</mark> · <mark class="example-chip">There’s a café next to the station.</mark> · <mark class="example-chip">There are two free seats.</mark>

07 · Small words, big meaning: fixed expressions 💬

  • be right / be wrong — <mark class="example-chip">You’re right.</mark> <mark class="example-chip">I’m wrong.</mark>
  • be afraid of / be sure — <mark class="example-chip">She’s afraid of dogs.</mark> <mark class="example-chip">I’m sure it’s okay.</mark>
  • be interested in / be good at — <mark class="example-chip">He’s interested in music.</mark> <mark class="example-chip">She’s good at English.</mark>
  • be late / early / ready / busy / free — <mark class="example-chip">We’re late.</mark> <mark class="example-chip">Dinner is ready.</mark> <mark class="example-chip">I’m free tomorrow.</mark>

🔎 Notice the prepositions that belong with some expressions: <strong>interested in</strong>, <strong>good at</strong>, <strong>afraid of</strong>. Learn them as little word groups.

08 · One step further: BE can be a helping verb 🟣 A2+

So far, <strong>be</strong> has carried the main meaning: <em>She is happy.</em> Sometimes, <strong>be</strong> helps another part of the sentence instead. You can recognise these patterns now and learn them gradually.

Helping verb patterns table for verb BE
PatternExampleWhat BE is doing
be + -ingShe is working.Helps form the present continuous: an action happening around now.
be + past participleThe table is made of wood.Helps form the passive: the sentence focuses on the thing or action, not the doer.

⚖️ <strong>Compare:</strong> <em>She is happy</em> = <strong>be</strong> describes her state. · <em>She is working</em> = <strong>is</strong> helps form the action verb <em>working</em>.

09 · Quick check 🎯

❓ Choose the correct sentence about age and a job.

Use be for age and put a/an before a singular countable job: She is 25. She is a doctor.

❓ Choose the correct positive short answer: “Is she at home?”

Positive short answers use the full form: Yes, she is.

❓ Complete the sentence: “___ there any cafés near here?”

Use are with the plural noun cafés: Are there any cafés near here?

❓ Which sentence describes an action happening around now?

In He is working, is + working forms the present continuous. In the other sentences, is describes a state or identity.

🌱 <strong>You do not have to master every pattern today.</strong> Start by noticing <strong>am / is / are</strong>, then practise one use at a time. The more examples you meet, the more natural BE will feel.

Verb 2 — "To Have" (have / has)

The verb <strong>to have</strong> is one of the most essential verbs in English. At A0–A1 level, you use it to talk about possessions, family, physical characteristics, food and drink, daily activities, and health conditions.

There are two essential present simple forms to learn first: • <strong>have</strong> — I, you, we, they • <strong>has</strong> — he, she, it

1. Forms & Conjugation Table (Present Simple)

Present simple conjugation and usage table for verb HAVE
SubjectAffirmative (+)Negative (-)Question (?)
I / You / We / TheyI have a phone. / You have a nice house. / We have two children. / They have a new car.I don't have a car. / You don't have a pen. / We don't have time. / They don't have any pets.Do I have time? / Do you have a question? / Do we have class today? / Do they have tickets?
He / She / ItHe has a sister. / She has blue eyes. / It has four legs.He doesn't have a job. / She doesn't have a brother. / It doesn't have a name.Does he have a pet? / Does she have a car? / Does it have a camera?
⚠️ Common Trap — Third Person Conjugation

Use has only with he, she, and it in positive present simple sentences.

INSTEAD OF ❌ Incorrect Form
TRY THIS ✅ Correct Form
She have a cat.
She has a cat.

2. Main Usages & Sentence Frames

  • A. Possessions: <mark class="example-chip">I have a new laptop.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She has a red bicycle.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">We have a big house in Paris.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">They have a friendly dog.</mark>
  • B. Family & Relationships: <mark class="example-chip">I have two brothers.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She has a young daughter.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">He has a big family.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">We have many good friends.</mark>
  • C. Physical Characteristics: <mark class="example-chip">I have brown eyes.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She has long blonde hair.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">He has a dark beard.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">The cat has a long tail.</mark>
  • D. Things & Features: <mark class="example-chip">The apartment has three bedrooms.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">The car has four doors.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">My phone has a great camera.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">The school has a large library.</mark>
  • E. Problems & Needs: <mark class="example-chip">I have a problem with my laptop.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She has a bad headache.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">We have an urgent question.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">He has a lot of work today.</mark>
  • F. Time & Availability: <mark class="example-chip">I have free time this afternoon.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">We have an important meeting today.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She has an English lesson at 10 AM.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">They have no money.</mark>
  • G. Food & Drink (Action Meaning): <mark class="example-chip">I have a delicious sandwich for lunch.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She has a cup of tea every morning.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">We have breakfast at 8 o'clock.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">They have dinner together at home.</mark> (Here have means eat or drink).

3. Negative Sentences (-) & The Base Verb Rule

To make negative sentences with <em>have</em>, use <strong>don't have</strong> or <strong>doesn't have</strong>.

Negative structure table for verb HAVE
SubjectNegative StructureExample Sentence
I / You / We / Theydon't haveI don't have any money. / We don't have class today.
He / She / Itdoesn't haveShe doesn't have a brother. / My phone doesn't have a good camera.
⚠️ Common Trap — Negative Base Verb Rule

After doesn't, always use the base form have, never has.

INSTEAD OF ❌ Incorrect Form
TRY THIS ✅ Correct Form
She doesn't has a car.
She doesn't have a car.

4. Yes/No Questions (?) & Short Answers

Yes and No questions and short answers table for verb HAVE

When you use does, the main verb returns to its basic infinitive form, have.

INSTEAD OF ❌ Incorrect Form
TRY THIS ✅ Correct Form
Does she has a cat?
Does she have a cat?

5. Wh- Questions with "Have"

  • What: <mark class="example-chip">What do you have in your bag?</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">What do you have for breakfast?</mark>
  • Who: <mark class="example-chip">Who do you have in your family?</mark>
  • How many: <mark class="example-chip">How many brothers do you have?</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">How many bedrooms does the house have?</mark>
  • How much: <mark class="example-chip">How much money do you have?</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">How much time do we have?</mark>
  • What colour: <mark class="example-chip">What colour eyes does she have?</mark>
  • What kind: <mark class="example-chip">What kind of phone does he have?</mark>

6. "Have got" vs "Do/Does Have"

Have got is common in informal English (especially British English) for possession, family, and physical features.

NEVER MIX THE TWO SYSTEMS:

INSTEAD OF ❌ Incorrect Form
TRY THIS ✅ Correct Form

7. Everyday Expressions & Illnesses with "Have"

  • Daily Activities & Routines: <mark class="example-chip">have breakfast / lunch / dinner</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a coffee</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a shower / bath</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a break</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a rest</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a party</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a good time</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a look</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a chat</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a holiday</mark>.
  • Illnesses & Physical Conditions: <mark class="example-chip">have a headache</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a stomachache</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a cold</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a fever</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">have a toothache</mark>.
  • Countable vs Uncountable (some / any): • Affirmative (+): <mark class="example-chip">I have some apples.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">I have some water.</mark> • Negative (-): <mark class="example-chip">I don't have any apples.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">I don't have any money.</mark> • Question (?): <mark class="example-chip">Do you have any questions?</mark>

Verb 3 — "To Think"

To Think allows you to express your opinion, share your thoughts, and ask others for their view.

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Present Simple Forms: I/You/We/They think | He/She/It thinks.
  • Expressing Opinions (I think that...): <mark class="example-chip">I think it is a good idea.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">I think learning English is fun.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She thinks this movie is great.</mark>
  • Short Responses: <mark class="example-chip">I think so.</mark> (I agree / I believe so) | <mark class="example-chip">I don't think so.</mark> (I disagree).
  • Asking Opinions: <mark class="example-chip">What do you think about this idea?</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">What do you think of this film?</mark>
  • Key Collocations: <mark class="example-chip">think twice before deciding</mark> (be careful), <mark class="example-chip">think about the problem</mark> (consider), <mark class="example-chip">think of a solution</mark> (have an opinion/idea).

Verb 4 — "To Know"

The verb <strong>to know</strong> is essential for talking about facts, information, people, places, skills, and things we understand. In our logical sequence: first we think, and then we know!

The primary present simple forms are: • <strong>know</strong> — I, you, we, they • <strong>knows</strong> — he, she, it

1. Forms & Conjugation Table (Present Simple)

Present simple conjugation and usage table for verb KNOW
SubjectAffirmative (+)Negative (-)Question (?)
I / You / We / TheyI know the answer. / You know my brother. / We know this city well. / They know the truth.I don't know his name. / You don't know this place. / We don't know what to do. / They don't know anyone here.Do I know you? / Do you know the way? / Do we know the rules? / Do they know the price?
He / She / ItHe knows the teacher. / She knows the answer. / It knows the route.He doesn't know my address. / She doesn't know the truth. / It doesn't know any tricks.Does he know the time? / Does she know Peter? / Does it know where to go?
⚠️ Common Trap — Third Person Rule

Add -s to know only with he, she, and it in affirmative sentences.

INSTEAD OF ❌ Incorrect Form
TRY THIS ✅ Correct Form
She know the answer.
She knows the answer.

2. Facts, Information & Embedded Question Words

  • A. Facts & Direct Information (Subject + know/knows + noun): • <mark class="example-chip">I know the correct answer to question 5.</mark> • <mark class="example-chip">She knows his full name and phone number.</mark> • <mark class="example-chip">He knows the truth about what happened.</mark> • <mark class="example-chip">We know the safety rules at work.</mark>
  • B. Information with Question Words: • <mark class="example-chip">I know where he lives in London.</mark> • <mark class="example-chip">She knows what you want for your birthday.</mark> • <mark class="example-chip">We know when the English lesson starts.</mark> • <mark class="example-chip">They know why he is angry today.</mark>

3. Familiarity with People & Places

  • A. People: <mark class="example-chip">I know Anna from university.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She knows my older brother.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">Do you know this man?</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">We don't know those people over there.</mark>
  • B. Places: <mark class="example-chip">I know this city very well.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">He knows London like the back of his hand.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">Do you know this Italian restaurant?</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">They don't know the area.</mark>
⚠️ Distinction — Knowing Someone

In English, knowing someone means you are familiar with them.
• I know Peter. = I am familiar with him.
• I meet Peter every day. = I encounter/see him every day.
• I know Peter well. = I am very familiar with him.

4. Negative Sentences (-) & "I don't know"

Use <strong>don't know</strong> or <strong>doesn't know</strong>. After <em>doesn't</em>, always use the base verb <strong>know</strong>, never <em>knows</em>.

INSTEAD OF ❌ Incorrect Form
TRY THIS ✅ Correct Form
He doesn't knows.
He doesn't know.

The Essential Phrase — "I don't know":
• “What's his name?” — “I don't know.”
• “Where is my phone?” — “I don't know.”
• Useful Variations: I really don't know. / I don't know yet. / I don't know why. / I don't know anything about it.

5. Yes/No & Wh- Questions (Embedded Word Order)

In embedded questions with <em>Do you know...?</em>, use <strong>statement word order</strong> (Subject + Verb).

INSTEAD OF ❌ Incorrect Order
TRY THIS ✅ Statement Order
Do you know where is the station?
Do you know where the station is?
Do you know what time is it?
Do you know what time it is?
Do you know who is she?
Do you know who she is?

6. "Know that" & "Know how to" (Skills)

  • A. Know (that) + sentence: <mark class="example-chip">I know that you are tired.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She knows that he is French.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">We know English is important.</mark> ('that' is optional in spoken English). • Negative contrast: <mark class="example-chip">I don't know that he is here.</mark> (lack of awareness) vs <mark class="example-chip">I don't know if he is here.</mark> (uncertainty).
  • B. Skills (know how to + base verb): • <mark class="example-chip">I know how to swim.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She knows how to cook Italian food.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">He knows how to drive a car.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">They don't know how to play chess.</mark>
  • C. Know vs Know about: • <mark class="example-chip">I know Maria.</mark> = I am acquainted with Maria personally. • <mark class="example-chip">I know about computers.</mark> = I have knowledge on the topic of computers.

7. Stative Verb Rule & Comparisons

⚠️ Stative Verb Warning

Know describes a mental state, so it is not used in continuous tenses (-ing).

INSTEAD OF ❌
TRY THIS ✅
I am knowing the answer.
I know the answer.

To talk about gradual familiarity, say: I am getting to know him.

Comparison table for mental state verbs think, know, and understand

8. Key Daily Expressions with "Know"

  • let someone know = inform me (<mark class="example-chip">Let me know when you arrive!</mark>)
  • get to know someone = gradually become familiar (<mark class="example-chip">I want to get to know you.</mark>)
  • as far as I know = to my knowledge (<mark class="example-chip">As far as I know, the café is open.</mark>)
  • know by heart = memorize completely (<mark class="example-chip">I know this poem by heart.</mark>)
  • know for sure = be 100% certain (<mark class="example-chip">I don't know for sure.</mark>)

9. Check Your Understanding

❓ Which sentence has correct word order after 'Do you know...?'

In embedded questions after 'Do you know...?', use normal statement word order: 'where the station is'.

❓ Choose the correct skill structure: 'She knows ___ driving a car.'

For skills, use 'know how to + base verb': 'She knows how to drive a car.'

Verb 5 — "To Like" ❤️

After learning <em>to be</em>, <em>to have</em>, <em>to think</em>, and <em>to know</em>, let's master <strong>to like</strong>! This is a very useful verb for talking about things you enjoy, your hobbies, food, music, people, and preferences.

1. Preference Scale: Love, Like, Dislike & Hate

  • ❤️ **Love (❤️❤️❤️❤️❤️):** Enjoy very much ("I love chocolate!")
  • ❤️ **Like (❤️❤️❤️):** Enjoy ("I like chocolate.")
  • 👎 **Dislike (👎):** Not enjoy ("I dislike chocolate." — Note: *Dislike* sounds formal; in daily English, people usually say "I don't like chocolate.")
  • 😠 **Hate (😠):** Dislike very strongly ("I hate cold weather." — Note: Be careful using *hate* with people, as it sounds harsh).

2. Conjugation Table & The Third-Person Rule

To like is a regular verb. In the present simple, add -s for he, she, and it.

Present simple conjugation and usage table for verb LIKE

⚠️ Common Trap — Third-Person -s Rule

Add -s with he, she, and it in positive sentences.

INSTEAD OF ❌
TRY THIS ✅
She like pizza.
She likes pizza.

In negatives and questions, after doesn't or does, the main verb returns to base like:

INSTEAD OF ❌
TRY THIS ✅
She doesn't likes tea. / Does he likes football?
She doesn't like tea. / Does he like football?

3. What Can Come After "Like"?

  • Pattern 1: Like + Noun: <mark class="example-chip">I like Italian food.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She likes dogs.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">They like classical music.</mark>
  • Pattern 2: Like + Verb-ing: <mark class="example-chip">I like swimming in the morning.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">He likes cooking pasta.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">We like watching films.</mark> (Emphasizes active enjoyment)
  • Pattern 3: Like + to + Verb: <mark class="example-chip">I like to read before bed.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She likes to dance.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">They like to travel in summer.</mark> (Emphasizes habit or preference)
⚠️ Distinction — Like vs Would Like

• I like coffee. = I enjoy coffee generally.
• I'd like a coffee. = I want a coffee now, politely. (Would like is covered on To Want!).

4. Related Verbs: Love, Dislike & Hate

The same grammar rules apply to <em>love</em>, <em>dislike</em>, and <em>hate</em>:<br>• <mark class="example-chip">I love my family.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She loves dancing.</mark><br>• <mark class="example-chip">He dislikes waiting.</mark> (Or naturally: <mark class="example-chip">He doesn't like waiting.</mark>)<br>• <mark class="example-chip">My cat hates water.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">They hate getting up early.</mark>

5. Everyday Expressions & Stative Verb Rule

  • Intensity: <mark class="example-chip">I really like it!</mark> (A lot) | <mark class="example-chip">I quite like it.</mark> (To some degree) | <mark class="example-chip">I don't like it very much.</mark>
  • Polite Neutrality: <mark class="example-chip">I don't mind.</mark> (It is okay / no objection) | <mark class="example-chip">It's not my cup of tea.</mark> (Idiom: Not something I enjoy)
⚠️ Stative Verb Rule

Like describes a state or preference, so use present simple in standard English.

INSTEAD OF ❌
TRY THIS ✅
I'm liking this song.
I like this song.

6. Check Your Understanding

❓ Choose the correct sentence:

With 'she', use 'doesn't' followed by the base verb 'like' without an -s.

❓ What does 'I'd like a glass of water' mean?

'I'd like' means 'I would like', which expresses a polite desire right now, not a general hobby.

Verb 6 — "To Want / Would Like"

To Want and Would Like express desires, intentions, and polite requests.

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Direct Desires (Want + Noun / Verb): <mark class="example-chip">I want a glass of cold water.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">I want to learn English fluently.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">Where do you want to go tonight?</mark>
  • Polite Standard (Would like + Noun / Verb): <mark class="example-chip">I would like to order lunch now, please.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She would like a fresh salad.</mark>
  • Negative Forms: <mark class="example-chip">I don't want to be late for class.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">He doesn't want to go home yet.</mark>
  • Common Phrases: <mark class="example-chip">if you want to come</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">I want to know the truth</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">we would like to see the museum</mark>.

Verb 7 — "To Go / Be Going To"

To Go expresses movement, travel, routines, and future intentions (be going to).

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Present Simple Forms: I/You/We/They go | He/She/It goes.
  • Movement & Prepositions: <mark class="example-chip">I go to work by train.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She goes to school every morning.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">We go to the park on Sundays.</mark>
  • Exceptions without 'to': <mark class="example-chip">go home</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">go abroad on holiday</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">go downtown</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">go grocery shopping</mark>.
  • Future Intentions (be going to + verb): <mark class="example-chip">I am going to eat dinner with friends.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">They are going to buy a new car next month.</mark>
  • Transport Phrases: <mark class="example-chip">go on foot</mark> (walk), <mark class="example-chip">go by bus</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">go by train</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">go by car</mark>.

Verb 8 — "To Can" (Modal Verb)

Can is a modal verb expressing ability, permission, and polite requests.

Forms & Sentence Frames

⚠️ Modal Rule

Can never changes form (no '-s' for he/she/it). It is followed directly by a base verb without 'to'.

  • Ability: <mark class="example-chip">I can speak three languages.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She can swim very fast in the pool.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">He can play the acoustic guitar.</mark>
  • Negative Ability (cannot / can't): <mark class="example-chip">I can't drive a manual car.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">She can't come to the party tonight.</mark>
  • Requests & Permission: <mark class="example-chip">Can you help me with this bag?</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">Can I have a glass of water?</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">You can sit here near the window.</mark>
  • Useful Phrases: <mark class="example-chip">as fast as I can</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">I can do it</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">Can I ask a quick question?</mark>

</section>

Verb 9 — "To Eat"

To Eat is used for food, daily meals, dining habits, and food preferences.

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Present Simple Forms: I/You/We/They eat | He/She/It eats.

  • Questions & Desires: <mark class="example-chip">What do you want to eat for dinner?</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">Do you eat vegetarian food?</mark>
  • Phrasal Verbs & Collocations: <mark class="example-chip">eat out at an Italian restaurant</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">eat healthy food</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">let's find something to eat</mark>.

Verb 10 — "To Drink"

To Drink covers beverages, hydration, offering drinks, and social café situations.

Forms & Sentence Frames

  • Present Simple Forms: I/You/We/They drink | He/She/It drinks.
  • Daily Beverages: <mark class="example-chip">I drink two litres of water every day.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">He drinks black coffee in the morning.</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">We drink hot tea in winter.</mark>
  • Offers & Polite Phrases: <mark class="example-chip">Would you like something to drink?</mark> / <mark class="example-chip">What would you like to drink with your meal?</mark>
  • Noun Usage: <mark class="example-chip">a hot drink on a cold day</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">a cold drink in summer</mark>, <mark class="example-chip">let's have a drink together</mark>.
