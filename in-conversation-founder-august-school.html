In Conversation: Founder James on the Fear of Lesson One and Unlearning School Trauma

Why do articulate, accomplished adults freeze at the classroom door? Founder James (JY DM) deconstructs childhood red ink, the myth of grammatical readiness, and how speaking from minute one heals the learning brain.

J Written by JY DM
📅 2026-08-01 ⏱️ 7 min read A2–C1

Every August, as stationery shops fill with crisp notebooks and sharp pencils, millions of adults feel a phantom knot tighten in their stomach. It is the lingering echo of school: the fear of being called upon, the terror of red ink across a margins, and the paralyzing belief that one must study in secret until one is 'ready'. In our inaugural Vol. 2026.08 editorial feature, COSYmagazine sits down exclusively with founder James (JY DM) to discuss why adult language learning must begin with unlearning, and how opening our mouths on Day One transforms everything.

Part I: The Ghost of the Red Pen

COSYmagazine: James, when adults enter a language classroom, they aren't just bringing curiosity; they're bringing thirty years of conditioning. What is the single biggest misconception about learning a language that school instilled in us?

James: School taught us that a mistake is a moral failing. When you conjugate a verb incorrectly in high school, a teacher strikes through it with a red pen, subtracts two points, and hands back a paper that essentially whispers: you are inadequate. That creates an adult who treats silence as safety. But language is an acoustic and biological muscle, not an arithmetic exam. In real human conversation, a mistake is not a failure; it is diagnostic data. It tells your interlocutor where to support you and tells your brain where the next synaptic bridge needs to be built.

“School taught us to stay silent until we were perfect. Life teaches us that perfection never speaks.”

— James (JY DM), Founder of COSY Languages

Part II: The Myth of 'Preparation'

COSYmagazine: Many learners say, 'Let me just finish this grammar book, or get to B1 on an app, and then I'll book a conversation lesson.' Why is that instinct so counter-productive?

James: Because passive recognition and active motor production live in completely different neuro-anatomical networks. When you read a grammar table, your visual cortex recognizes patterns. But when a human looks you in the eye and asks what you had for breakfast, your amygdala reacts to social exposure, your vocal chords must coordinate twenty muscles, and your working memory must retrieve words under emotional pressure. If you wait until you 'feel ready', you will wait forever. You do not learn to swim by reading water chemistry on the deck. You get wet on minute one, while the water is still shallow.

"Teacher, is it 'in' or 'at'?" → "Say the whole story first; we'll polish the preposition at the end."

The speaking-first priority: meaning and vocal projection precede microscopic syntax correction.

Context: Founder's core teaching reflex in lesson one.

Part III: How to Design an Anxiety-Free Lesson

COSYmagazine: How does the COSY master curriculum protect the student's affective filter in that terrifying first twenty minutes?

James: By lowering the cognitive stakes to zero. We never ask open-ended, intimidating questions like 'Tell me about yourself'. Instead, we give what we call sentence frames. We give them 80% of the sentence on a silver platter, and ask them to insert just one word: their city, their coffee preference, their favourite dog breed. The moment their voice produces a true, authentic statement in French, Italian, Greek, or Russian, the brain gets a shot of dopamine. The monster under the bed disappears.

Part IV: The Vision for COSYmagazine

COSYmagazine: What is your hope for readers holding this inaugural August issue in their hands or opening it in our flipbook?

James: I want them to experience language learning not as a cold chore or a high-stakes exam, but as a rich cultural salon. Like flipping through Vogue, every page should delight the eye, nourish the mind with philosophy and psychology, and make them whisper a new sentence out loud before turning to the next page. If a reader closes this issue and says 'Bonjour', 'Ciao', or 'Γεια σου' to someone without apologizing for their accent, our work is done.
