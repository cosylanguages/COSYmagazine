Welcome to Part 2 of our 2-part core verb series for A0–A1 and A2 English learners! (Read Part 1 if you missed it). Here we cover 10 core action, perception, and modal verbs. All conjugation tables and verb forms are consultable on COSYtools.

Ten verbs are enough to ask, answer, wait, listen, come and leave, and get things done on day one. They are also the verbs where English learners make the most predictable mistakes: do or make, say or tell, look or watch, come or leave. The good news is that the mistakes follow patterns, and patterns can be learned.

Read this article out loud. For each verb, start with the table and say the forms without looking. Then read the three sentences in the 'Say aloud' section, hide them, and repeat them from memory, changing one word to talk about your own life. Finish with the mistake card: it shows the sentence many beginners say, and the one to say instead. Speak first, live the rule second: it is faster and more natural.

“A verb learned in a sentence is worth ten verbs learned in a list.”

The memory map: ten verbs, five functions

Instead of learning ten verbs one after another, sort them by function. When you want to say something, first ask: am I acting, communicating, perceiving, obliged, or exchanging? The verb comes almost by itself.

  • Acting & making: do / make. Activities, tasks, creation.
  • Communicating: speak / talk / say / tell, ask / answer. Languages, conversations, questions.
  • Perceiving: look / watch / see, listen / hear. Vision and hearing.
  • Obligation & advice: must / have to / should. Rules, necessity, recommendation.
  • Movement & exchange: come / leave, take / give. Arriving, departing, transferring.
  • Life & patience: live, wait. Home, dwelling, waiting.

The 10 Core Verbs in Depth (Part 2)

1. Do / Make

Do and make both translate as one verb in many languages, which is why they cause so many mistakes. A simple rule of thumb: do is for activities, tasks and work that already exist (homework, the dishes, a job). Make is for creating or producing something new (a meal, a plan, a mistake, a decision).

Pronoundo (present)make (present)Example
I / you / we / theydomakeWe make dinner and do the dishes.
He / she / itdoesmakesShe does her homework.
Past (all)didmadeHe made a cake yesterday.
Past participledonemadeI have done my work.
-ing formdoingmakingWhat are you doing?
  • Activities & chores: 'do the shopping', 'do the laundry', 'do your best'.
  • Creating & producing: 'make a cake', 'make a call', 'make money'.
  • Common phrases: 'do business', 'do research' | 'make progress', 'make a mistake'.

Say aloud

What do you do on Sundays?

In this question, 'do' appears twice: the helper and the main verb. The answer is an activity.

Context: A classmate asks about your weekend.

I make coffee every morning before work.

Make = you create something (coffee) that did not exist before.

Context: You describe your morning routine.

Can you do me a favour? I need to make an appointment.

'Do a favour' is fixed. 'Make an appointment' is also fixed.

Context: You ask a friend for help.

Mini-dialogue: A: What are you doing? B: I'm making lunch. Can you do the dishes later? A: Sure, no problem!

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

01

Do or make

✗ Not quite

'I did a mistake.'

✓ Say this instead

'I made a mistake.'

Why this mistake happens. Your language probably uses one verb for both. English splits them, and the split is partly by habit, so you must learn the pairs together.

The COSY rule. A mistake, a decision, a plan, a noise, a choice and a phone call are all made. Homework, housework, an exam (to take) and a favour are done.

More examples
  • I made a decision.
  • She did her homework.
  • We made a plan.

❓ I need to ___ a phone call.

A phone call is 'made': 'I need to make a phone call.'

2. Speak / Talk / Say / Tell

Four verbs, one idea: communication. They are not interchangeable. Speak is about the language or a formal talk. Talk is a conversation. Say is about the exact words. Tell needs a person to receive the information.

VerbMeaningTypical structureExample
speakuse a language / voicespeak + languageI speak English and Spanish.
talkhave a conversationtalk to / with + personWe talk every day.
saypronounce wordssay + wordsShe says 'hello'.
tellgive informationtell + person + thingTell me the truth.
Past formsspoke, talked, said, toldHe told me a story.
  • Languages & formal speech: 'speak Italian', 'speak slowly'.
  • Conversation: 'talk about the weather', 'talk to a friend'.
  • Common phrases: 'speak fluently', 'talk with', 'say yes', 'tell a story'.

Say aloud

Do you speak French? – A little.

Speak + a language.

Context: A stranger asks for help in the street.

Can we talk after class?

Talk = a conversation between people.

Context: You ask your teacher for a minute.

He said he was tired, so I told him to rest.

Say = the words. Tell = tell someone something.

Context: You report what happened.

Mini-dialogue: A: Can I talk to you for a minute? B: Of course. What do you want to say? A: Tell me honestly: do you speak Spanish?

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

02

Say or tell

✗ Not quite

'She said me the answer.'

✓ Say this instead

'She told me the answer.'

Why this mistake happens. In many languages the same verb works with or without a listener. English 'say' cannot be followed directly by a person.

The COSY rule. Tell + person + information. Say + words (and 'to' + person if needed): 'She said to me' is possible but rare.

More examples
  • He told me the news.
  • She said hello.
  • I told them to wait.

❓ He ___ me a funny story.

After a person, we use tell: 'He told me a story.'

3. Look / Watch / See

English separates three kinds of vision. See happens by itself. Look is deliberate and directed. Watch is following something that moves or changes over time.

VerbMeaningTypical structureExample
seenotice with the eyessee + objectI see a bird.
lookdirect your eyeslook at + objectLook at this picture!
watchfollow with attentionwatch + TV / a matchWe watch a film.
Past formssaw, looked, watchedShe watched the match.
Participlesseen, looked, watchedHave you seen my keys?
  • Seeing: 'I can't see without my glasses'.
  • Looking: 'Look at the board', 'Look! A rainbow!'.
  • Common phrases: 'look for', 'look forward to', 'watch out', 'see you soon'.

Say aloud

Look at the blackboard, please.

Look needs 'at' before the object.

Context: Your teacher is explaining a new word.

We watch a film every Friday night.

Watch = follow a moving image for a while.

Context: You describe your weekend.

I can see the sea from my window.

See = it enters your sight without effort.

Context: You describe your flat.

Mini-dialogue: A: Look! There's a cat on the roof. B: Where? I can't see it. A: Watch, it's going to jump! B: Oh, I see it now.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

03

Look at, not look

✗ Not quite

'Look the picture.'

✓ Say this instead

'Look at the picture.'

Why this mistake happens. Many languages use a single verb with a direct object. English 'look' always needs a preposition.

The COSY rule. Look at = direct your eyes. Look for = search. Look forward to = wait with pleasure.

More examples
  • Look at me.
  • I'm looking for my keys.
  • We look forward to the holidays.

❓ Can you ___ a bird in the tree?

'Can you see...?' is the natural way to ask if something is visible.

4. Listen / Hear

Hear is passive: the sound arrives, you did not choose it. Listen is active: you pay attention on purpose, and it needs to before the thing you listen to.

VerbPresentPastExample
I / you / we / theyhear / listenheard / listenedI hear a noise.
He / she / ithears / listensheard / listenedShe listens to music.
-ing formhearing / listeningI'm listening!
Past participleheard / listenedHave you heard the news?
QuestionDo you hear me?Are you listening to me?
  • Listening: 'listen to the radio', 'listen to your teacher'.
  • Hearing: 'I hear the rain', 'I can't hear you'.
  • Common phrases: 'listen carefully', 'hear from', 'glad to hear that'.

Say aloud

Listen to this song, it's amazing!

Listen + to + object.

Context: You share your favourite music with a friend.

I can't hear you. Can you speak louder?

Hear = the sound reaches your ears.

Context: A bad phone line.

It was nice to hear from you!

Hear from = receive news from a person.

Context: You answer an old friend's email.

Mini-dialogue: A: Did you hear that noise? B: No, I wasn't listening. A: Listen carefully... there! B: Oh yes, I hear it now.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

04

Listen to

✗ Not quite

'I listen music every day.'

✓ Say this instead

'I listen to music every day.'

Why this mistake happens. Your language may use the verb directly with the object. English needs a small word, and it is easy to forget when you speak fast.

The COSY rule. Listen + to. Hear needs no preposition. 'Listen' is deliberate, 'hear' is automatic.

More examples
  • Listen to me.
  • I hear a bird.
  • They listen to the radio.

❓ I like to ___ to music.

Listen to + object. 'Hear' does not take 'to'.

📌 Listening in a noisy world

English speakers often say 'I'm all ears' to show they are listening closely, and 'I hear you' to mean 'I understand how you feel', even if they do not agree. When you answer on the phone, a short 'Right', 'I see' or 'Mm-hm' shows you are listening. Silence can sound as if you disconnected, so keep making small sounds. Politely ask people to repeat: 'Sorry, could you say that again?' is always welcome, and native speakers will often slow down for you.

5. Must / Have To / Should

All three verbs express obligation or advice, with clear differences in strength. Must is strong personal obligation or written rule ('You must stop'). Have to is an outside requirement ('I have to work'). Should is friendly advice or recommendation ('You should sleep'). In the negative, mustn't means forbidden, while don't have to means not necessary.

ModalMeaningExampleNegative
mustStrong obligation / ruleI must renew my passport.mustn't (forbidden)
have toOutside necessityI have to work tomorrow.don't have to (optional)
shouldAdvice / recommendationYou should see a doctor.shouldn't (bad idea)
  • Rules & duty: 'You must wear a seatbelt', 'Citizens must follow the law'.
  • Daily necessity: 'I have to wake up at seven', 'Do you have to go now?'.
  • Friendly advice: 'You should drink more water', 'We should leave early'.

Say aloud

I have to get up early tomorrow.

Have to = an obligation from outside (work, school).

Context: You explain why you can't stay out late.

You mustn't use your phone in the exam.

Mustn't = it is forbidden.

Context: The teacher explains the rules.

You look tired; you should go to bed early, but you don't have to finish the chores tonight.

Should = good advice. Don't have to = not necessary.

Context: You invite a friend to dinner.

Mini-dialogue: A: I have a bad headache. B: You should rest and drink water. A: But I have to finish this report! B: You mustn't push yourself too hard.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

05

Mustn't or don't have to

✗ Not quite

'You mustn't pay, it's free.'

✓ Say this instead

'You don't have to pay, it's free.'

Why this mistake happens. In many languages, a single negative covers both 'forbidden' and 'not necessary'. English separates them sharply.

The COSY rule. Mustn't = forbidden. Don't have to = not necessary. After both, the verb has no 'to': 'You must go', 'You have to go'.

More examples
  • You mustn't smoke here.
  • You don't have to come.
  • She doesn't have to cook.

❓ Entry is free: you ___ buy a ticket.

It is not necessary, so 'don't have to'.

6. Come / Leave

Come and leave are the twin verbs of movement. Come means motion towards the speaker or towards a destination ('Come to my house'). Leave means departing from a place ('I leave home at eight') or leaving something behind ('Don't leave your umbrella'). Both are irregular: come, came, come; leave, left, left.

VerbPresentPastParticipleExample
comecome / comescamecomeShe came to visit us yesterday.

-ing formcoming / leavingAre you coming with us?
Directioncome to + placeleave + placeleave for + destinationHe left for London.
  • Arrivals & invitations: 'come in', 'come home', 'come from Spain'.
  • Departures: 'leave the house', 'leave early', 'leave for work'.
  • Common phrases: 'come back', 'come on', 'leave me alone', 'leave a message'.

Say aloud

Where do you come from? – I come from Italy.

Come from = origin or nationality.

Context: Meeting someone new in class.

What time do you leave home in the morning?

Leave + place directly, no preposition.

Context: Talking about daily routines.

She came back late and left early the next morning.

Irregular past forms: came and left.

Context: Describing a busy travel schedule.

Mini-dialogue: A: When are you coming to visit? B: I can come on Saturday! A: What time do you leave your city? B: I leave at eight and arrive at ten.

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner.

06

Leave from or leave

✗ Not quite

'I leave from home at eight.'

✓ Say this instead

'I leave home at eight.'

Why this mistake happens. Many languages use a preposition of origin ('partir de'). English uses 'leave' directly with the place of departure.

The COSY rule. Leave + place (no 'from'): 'I leave work at 5'. Use leave for + destination: 'I leave for Paris'.

More examples
  • She left the office.
  • We leave for the airport now.
  • He leaves home early.

❓ What time do you ___ home for school?

We say 'leave home', without 'from'.

📌 Saying goodbye and leaving politely

When leaving a gathering in English-speaking cultures, it is polite to give advance notice: 'I'm afraid I have to leave soon' or 'I should get going'. Slipping away without saying goodbye ('an Irish exit') can be considered informal, whereas thanking your host with 'Thank you for having me' leaves a warm impression.

7. Take / Get / Give

Take and give are fundamental opposites of exchange. Take brings an object towards yourself or along with you ('Take an umbrella'). Give transfers something to another person ('Give him the keys'). Both have irregular forms: take, took, taken; give, gave, given.

07

Give to me or give me

✗ Not quite

'Give to me the salt, please.'

✓ Say this instead

'Give me the salt, please.'

Why this mistake happens. English prefers the double object order: give + person + thing. When using 'to', the object comes first: 'Give the salt to me'.

The COSY rule. Give + person + thing ('Give me the book') OR give + thing + to + person ('Give the book to me').

More examples
  • Give me your phone number.
  • She gave him a warm smile.
  • Give the ticket to the conductor.

❓ Can you ___ me a glass of water?

Pattern: 'give + person + thing' -> 'give me a glass'.

📌 Please, thank you and polite requests

English speakers say 'please' and 'thank you' far more often than learners expect: when ordering coffee, getting on a bus, or when someone passes you the salt. 'Could you give me...?' and 'Could I take a...?' are polite ways to ask for things in shops and cafés.

8. Ask / Answer

Ask and answer work in pairs: someone asks, someone answers. Ask is flexible: you ask a question, ask for something, or ask a person. Answer can be a verb or a noun.

Say aloud

Can I ask you a question?

Ask + person + question.

Context: You interrupt politely in class.

Could you answer the phone, please?

Answer the phone: it is a transitive verb here, no preposition.

Context: Your hands are full.

I asked for the bill, and the waiter answered quickly.

Ask for + thing.

Context: You tell a story about a restaurant.

Mini-dialogue: A: Excuse me, can I ask something? B: Sure, go ahead. A: Can you answer a question about the homework? B: Of course. Ask me!

Play both roles aloud, then swap.

Context: A short exchange to rehearse with a partner or in front of the mirror.

08

Ask for, answer to

✗ Not quite

'I answered to the teacher.'

✓ Say this instead

'I answered the teacher.'

Why this mistake happens. In many languages, 'answer' takes a preposition ('answer to'). In English, 'answer' is followed directly by the person or the question.

The COSY rule. Answer + object, no 'to'. Ask for + thing. Ask + person + question.

More examples
  • Answer the question.
  • She asked for water.
  • He asked me a question.

❓ Please ___ the phone, I'm busy.

Answer the phone: no preposition.

📌 Asking politely

Direct questions can sound sharp in English. Add 'Could you...?', 'Would you mind...?' or begin with 'Excuse me' before a stranger. A 'no' is often softened: 'I'm afraid I can't', 'I'd love to, but...'. When someone asks 'How are you?', they usually expect a short answer ('Fine, thanks, and you?'), not a detailed one. Learning these small phrases helps you ask and answer in a way that feels natural to native speakers.

9. Wait

Wait is a short verb with a big grammar trap: it needs for before the thing or person you expect. It also joins many time phrases.

09

Wait for

✗ Not quite

'I wait my friend.'

✓ Say this instead

'I wait for my friend.'

Why this mistake happens. In many languages, 'wait' takes a direct object. English needs 'for'. Without it, the sentence feels unfinished.

The COSY rule. Wait for + person or thing. Wait to + verb. Wait alone or with time: 'Wait a minute.'

More examples
  • Wait for me!
  • I'm waiting for an answer.
  • We wait to start.

❓ Please wait ___ me at the door.

Wait for + person: 'Please wait for me.'

📌 Queueing: a national art

In Britain, people queue patiently, in a straight line, and it is considered rude to jump the queue. Saying 'Sorry, I think I was next' politely works if someone cuts in front. In many English-speaking countries, 'wait your turn' is a basic rule of public life, in shops, buses and cafés. Learners can also use 'Excuse me, is this the end of the line?' to join a queue. 'Hold on' and 'Just a second' are friendly ways to ask for a little time.

10. Live

Live talks about where you have your home and how you spend your life. Notice the pronunciation: the verb live rhymes with 'give', but the adjective live (a live show) rhymes with 'five'.

10

Present tense with live

✗ Not quite

'I am living here since 2020.'

✓ Say this instead

'I have lived here since 2020.'

Why this mistake happens. Many languages use the present tense for situations that started in the past. English needs the present perfect with 'since' and 'for'.

The COSY rule. Live is a state: use the simple present for facts ('I live in Rome'). Use have lived with 'since' or 'for'.

More examples
  • I have lived here for five years.
  • She has lived in Paris since May.
  • They live in Berlin.

❓ We ___ in a small town.

With 'we', no -s: 'We live in a small town.'

Your 5-minute speaking routine

Five minutes a day, out loud, beat an hour of silent reading on Sunday. Here is a simple plan to repeat for one week: one minute per step, no more.

  1. Minute 1, warm-up: say the present, past and participle of do/make, come/leave, and take/give without looking at the tables.
  2. Minute 2, three verbs, three sentences: choose three verbs and say one true sentence about your life for each: 'I live...', 'I have to...', 'I wait for...'.
  3. Minute 3, mistake hunt: reread one 'Not quite' card and say the correct sentence three times, changing the subject each time.
  4. Minute 4, mini-dialogue: play both roles of a dialogue with 'Can I ask...?', 'Could you give me...?', 'Do you understand?', and record yourself on your phone.
  5. Minute 5, replay: listen to the recording and pick one sentence to keep for tomorrow. Write it on a note near your desk.
