Everyday Phrases and Natural Alternatives for B1–B2

Choose the right phrase for the situation: agreeing, giving opinions, thanking, apologising and more, with register notes, dialogues and practice.

Read in: 🇬🇷 EL 🇫🇷 FR 🇮🇹 IT 🇷🇺 RU 🇬🇧 EN
J Written by JY DM
📅 2026-09-13 ⏱️ 10 min read B1–B2 EPISODE 2

At B1–B2 you can hold conversations, but you may use the same few phrases every time. That is normal, and simple phrases are often the most natural: Thanks, I think so, Sorry about that. This guide gives you more choices, so you can match the phrase to the situation: who you are talking to, how strong your feeling is, and whether you are speaking or writing.

Three questions before you choose

  1. Who am I talking to? A friend, a colleague, a customer, a stranger?
  2. How strong is my opinion or feeling? Stronger words change the message.
  3. Am I speaking or writing? Some phrases sound stiff in conversation.

Register labels in this guide

  • Informal: with friends and family, in relaxed talk.
  • Neutral: safe in most situations.
  • Polite: softer and more careful, good with people you do not know well.
  • Formal: writing, emails and official meetings.

These labels are general guidance. Usage changes between countries, regions and generations, so listen to how people around you speak.

3. Yes, no and of course

Yes, no and of course
Everyday phraseAlternativeRegisterUse it when...
Yes.Exactly. / That's right.NeutralYou confirm what someone said.
No.Not really.Informal–neutralYou want a softer no.
No.I'm afraid not.PoliteYou refuse or give bad news politely.
No.I don't think so.NeutralYou are not completely sure.
Of course.Absolutely. / Definitely.NeutralYou answer with strong confidence.

Careful: Of course can sound impatient if the answer seems obvious: Of course I know! Check your tone.

Two short dialogues

At work (formal):

  • Manager: What's your take on the new plan?
  • Sam: From my point of view, it's a good idea, but it comes down to the budget.
  • Manager: I see it differently. I think we should wait.
  • Sam: That's true, you have a point. Could you clarify the timeline?

With friends (informal):

  • Ana: Do you like the new café?
  • Ben: Honestly, it's not really my thing. It's pretty loud.
  • Ana: Really? I'm a fan of the coffee.
  • Ben: Fair enough. Thanks for showing me, anyway.
  • Ana: No problem.

A natural upgrade in action

“Before: "I think this proposal is good. I agree with you, but it depends on the budget. In the future, I want to do this."”

“After: "In my opinion, this is a good proposal. You have a point, though it comes down to the budget. Down the road, I'd like to do this."”

The meaning stays the same. The phrases only fit the situation better.

Quiz: choose the best phrase

❓ In a work meeting your manager asks for your opinion. Which phrase fits best?

From my point of view is neutral to formal, so it suits a meeting.

❓ A friend thanks you for a small favour. A natural informal reply is:

No problem is informal and common for small favours.

❓ Which phrase often shows that you accept part of an argument?

You have a point often comes before but or though.

❓ Which reply is the most polite way to say you do not mind which restaurant?

I don't mind is polite and shows you are flexible. I don't care can sound rude.

❓ You cannot come to a meeting. In a formal email you write:

I'm unable to attend is formal and suitable for emails.

❓ Which phrase shows you are following the speaker?

I see tells the speaker you understand.

Now you try

  1. Pick three phrases from this guide each week. Use them in a speaking club, in messages or in writing.
  2. Take the work dialogue and rewrite it for friends. Which phrases change?
  3. Record yourself for one minute giving an opinion. Use one phrase for agreeing, one for doubt and one for a reaction.
